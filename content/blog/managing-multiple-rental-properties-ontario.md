One rental property feels manageable. You know the tenant by name. You handle the maintenance calls yourself. Tax time means one T776 form and one income line.

Then you add a second property. And a third. At some point — and it's different for every landlord — the math stops working. The hours pile up. Things get missed. You start dreading the phone.

This guide explains what actually changes when you scale from one to four properties, where most Ontario landlords hit the wall, and how to decide what to do about it.

---

## What Changes Between One and Four Properties

A single rental is a side task. You can hold the whole operation in your head. A maintenance issue comes up, you deal with it. Rent is late, you text the tenant. Tax time means pulling a folder and handing it to your accountant.

Four rentals is a small business. The volume is the same but the complexity compounds.

**What you're now tracking across four properties:**

- Four rent amounts with potentially different due dates
- Four sets of maintenance histories, appliance ages, and repair schedules
- Four tenants with different communication styles, different lease terms, and different concerns
- Four insurance policies, four property tax accounts, four utility arrangements
- Four annual rent increase notices, each requiring a valid [N1 form](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/06r17) served 90 days in advance
- Four sets of potential LTB filings if problems arise

None of these are hard in isolation. Combined, they produce a volume problem. You are no longer a landlord — you're operating a portfolio with no staff.

**The time reality looks like this:**

| Portfolio size | Estimated hours per month | What consumes the time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 property | 2–4 hours | Occasional calls, one rent collection, one repair |
| 2–3 properties | 6–10 hours | Overlapping maintenance, multiple rent follow-ups |
| 4–6 properties | 15–25 hours | Tenant screening, LTB compliance, records, coordination |
| 6+ properties | 30+ hours | Effectively a part-time job |

These are averages. A single difficult tenancy — one LTB filing, one unit in poor condition, one slow-paying tenant — can push any portfolio into the next tier for weeks at a time.

## The Six Breaking Points

Most landlords don't burn out all at once. They hit one of these six breaking points:

### 1. The maintenance overlap

One hot-water heater fails on a Friday. You're scheduling a plumber when another tenant calls about a broken furnace. By Sunday you're fielding a third call about a roof leak from last week's rain.

With one property, a bad week happens once in a while. With four, a bad week happens every month. Emergency maintenance at one property disrupts everything else — your job, your weekend, your other tenants who are now waiting longer for their own open requests.

Without a contractor network on standby, every repair becomes a search. You're calling people cold, waiting on callbacks, managing schedules across multiple properties. What should take two hours takes two days.

### 2. The late rent spiral

One tenant starts paying late. You send a reminder, it helps, they pay. Then they're late again. You issue an [N4 notice](/blog/n4-notice-ontario). They pay the same day — the N4 is voided. They're late the next month.

Each cycle takes an evening. Multiply by two tenants in this pattern across a four-property portfolio and you're spending a weekend a month on rent collection alone. Then you have to decide: do you void the N4 again, or proceed to an L1 filing? Do you escalate, or give them another month? Every decision has legal implications and requires time you don't have.

### 3. The screening shortcut

You have a vacancy at a bad time. The carrying costs are real. You speed through the reference check, skip the credit check, take the tenant's word on employment. Six months later you're filing with the [Landlord and Tenant Board](https://tribunalsontario.ca/ltb/).

Rushed screening is almost always a product of capacity. When you're stretched thin, due diligence is the first thing to go. A bad tenant in one unit can occupy 80% of your management time while the other three properties are neglected. See [Tenant Screening Red Flags](/blog/tenant-screening-red-flags) for what to protect regardless of how busy you are.

### 4. The records problem

Your accountant asks for a list of every repair expense from the past tax year. You have some receipts in email, some in a folder, one on a Post-it. Your mileage log is incomplete.

You're leaving deductions on the table because you couldn't track everything. Repairs, insurance premiums, property tax, a portion of your phone, property management software, vehicle mileage — all deductible, most untracked. This gets worse with each property you add. A proper [Ontario landlord record-keeping system](/blog/landlord-record-keeping-ontario) needs dedicated time to maintain — time that's already scarce.

### 5. The legal misstep

You issue a notice on the wrong form, or serve it the wrong way, or miss the N4 deadline by a week. The LTB dismisses your application. You start over.

Ontario's Residential Tenancies Act has exact rules for every notice type, service method, and timing requirement. One portfolio with one active problem is manageable. Multiple properties with overlapping issues — one late-rent file, one maintenance dispute, one end-of-tenancy notice — means multiple legal processes running simultaneously. Getting any one wrong restarts the clock by months.

### 6. The opportunity cost

The Friday afternoon you're fielding maintenance calls is the Friday afternoon you could be doing due diligence on your next acquisition.

Portfolio growth stalls when operations consume all your available time. Every hour spent chasing a repair contractor or writing a rent reminder is an hour not spent analyzing deals, building lender relationships, or improving existing units. Landlords who scale past four or five properties typically either build systems, hire help, or stop growing.

## What Gets Missed Without Systems

Most landlords don't lose money on one big mistake. They lose it on the small things that fall through the cracks when they're stretched.

**What goes missing first:**

- **Annual rent increase notices** — Issued late or skipped. You lose the increase for that cycle. Over five years across three properties, that's thousands of dollars in forgone rent.
- **Last month's rent deposit interest** — Ontario requires you to pay annual interest on the last month's rent deposit, calculated using the rent guideline rate. Most landlords have never done this. The tenant can file a T1 application at the LTB to recover it.
- **Lease renewal tracking** — A tenant's fixed term ends. You don't send any documentation. The tenancy converts to month-to-month automatically — which is legal — but the transition is often missed, and landlords sometimes try to act on an "expired" lease in ways not permitted under the RTA.
- **Move-out inspection coordination** — You skip the written move-out condition report. When the tenant leaves damage, you have no documented baseline to support a claim. Without a signed inspection form, the claim doesn't hold.
- **Property insurance reviews** — Your policy still covers the property, but the coverage limit hasn't been updated in three years. Replacement cost has increased. You're now underinsured.
- **Tax deduction tracking** — See [Rental Property Tax Deductions Ontario](/blog/rental-property-tax-deductions-ontario) for the full list of what's available. Most landlords with multiple properties are missing at least three categories.
- **Maintenance log continuity** — Without a written record of when appliances were serviced or replaced, you can't accurately forecast capital expenses. A furnace that's 18 years old looks the same in a spreadsheet as one that's 4 years old.

These are not catastrophic individually. Together they represent hundreds or thousands of dollars per year in missed money and accumulated risk.

## How to Know You're Past the Self-Managing Threshold

There is no universal unit count. Some landlords manage eight properties with tight systems and a reliable contractor network. Others struggle with two.

The threshold is about capacity and complexity, not just doors.

**You may be past the threshold if:**

- You regularly delay responding to tenant requests because you don't have time
- You've had at least one legal misstep — a void notice, a late filing — in the past year
- Your records are disorganized and you dread tax time
- A new vacancy feels like a crisis rather than a normal part of the business
- You've passed on an acquisition because managing more feels impossible
- Your tenants call you at all hours and you don't have a clear process for off-hours emergencies
- You've thought "I can't keep doing this" about self-managing more than twice in the past six months

None of these alone is definitive. Several together, consistently, mean your current approach has hit a ceiling.

## Your Two Options When You Hit the Wall

When self-managing stops working, landlords typically choose one of two paths.

### Option 1: Build systems

This means investing time upfront in creating the infrastructure that makes scaling possible. The four areas that need documented systems:

**Tenant communication.** All communication goes through email or a written channel — no texting. Every request acknowledged in writing within 24 hours. This creates a paper trail and sets clear expectations.

**Maintenance.** A roster of pre-vetted contractors (plumber, electrician, HVAC technician, handyman) who know your properties. Ideally people you've used before and who understand your standards. Repair requests go to one contact method, get logged, and get followed up in writing.

**Records.** One folder per property — physical or digital — containing the lease, move-in inspection, all correspondence, all repair invoices, and all notices served. Updated at every transaction. Reviewed before tax season and before any LTB filing.

**Rent and compliance tracking.** A calendar or spreadsheet showing every rent due date, every N1 window, every fixed-term expiry date, and every renewal decision point. Reviewed monthly.

Software that Ontario landlords use for this: Buildium, Rent Manager, TenantCloud, and AppFolio all have Canadian versions. Even a disciplined Google Sheets setup can work if you commit to maintaining it. The tool matters less than the consistency.

Building these systems works if you have the time to build them and are genuinely interested in the operational side of real estate. The systems take months to build and require ongoing maintenance. Most landlords who try this path underestimate how much time the setup actually takes — and discover they built the system, but then didn't follow it when things got busy.

### Option 2: Hire a property manager

A licensed property manager handles tenant placement, rent collection, maintenance coordination, legal compliance, and record-keeping. You receive a monthly owner statement and a direct deposit.

In the London and SW Ontario market, full management typically costs 8–12% of gross monthly rent. On a $1,800/month unit, that's $144–$216/month. On a four-unit portfolio averaging $1,800/month, that's roughly $575–$865/month total — in exchange for getting your evenings and weekends back.

That cost is reduced by:
- Management fees are fully deductible as a rental expense on your T776
- Vacancy periods are typically shorter when a dedicated team handles showings and screening
- Avoided legal errors (void notices, missed deadlines) each save months of process and hundreds in filing fees
- Deferred maintenance gets caught during routine inspections rather than as emergency calls

The right question is not "can I afford a property manager?" It is "how much is my time worth, and what is self-managing actually costing me per month when you count everything?"

## Common Mistakes Portfolio Landlords Make

These show up repeatedly in landlords who are trying to self-manage three or more properties without formal systems.

**Texting tenants instead of emailing.** Text conversations are hard to search, easy to lose, and difficult to produce at an LTB hearing. Every maintenance request, late rent notice, and entry notification should go through email.

**Using the same lease for years without updating it.** The Ontario Standard Lease is updated periodically, and RTA rule changes affect what clauses are enforceable. A lease from 2020 may contain provisions that no longer apply — or that create confusion during a dispute.

**Handling repairs yourself instead of using licensed contractors.** Beyond the time cost, DIY repairs on rental properties can create insurance complications. If an unlicensed repair leads to water damage or a fire, your insurer may deny the claim. Licensed contractors also produce invoices — which are deductible.

**Mixing rental income and expenses with personal accounts.** This creates a records nightmare at tax time and makes it harder to assess whether each property is actually cash-flowing. Separate accounts per property, or at minimum one dedicated rental account, makes everything cleaner.

**Not logging tenant communications.** A written log of who said what, when, and what was agreed to is your primary protection if a dispute reaches the LTB. Landlords who rely on memory — or text threads they've since deleted — are at a significant disadvantage at hearings.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**At what point should I stop self-managing?**
There is no fixed unit count, but most Ontario landlords reach a practical breaking point somewhere between three and five properties — when maintenance volume, legal compliance, and tenant management collectively start pulling them away from growth or quality of life. The signal is usually consistent stress, not a single bad month.

**Is property management worth it in London, Ontario?**
In the London and SW Ontario market, a professional manager with a reliable local contractor network typically fills vacancies in two to four weeks and catches compliance issues (rental licensing, property standards orders) before they become fines. The fee is usually offset by lower vacancy costs and avoided legal errors. Ask for itemized pricing — the percentage rate doesn't tell you what's included.

**Can I deduct property management fees on my taxes?**
Yes. Management fees paid to a licensed property manager are a deductible rental expense on your T776 Statement of Real Estate Rentals. This reduces the effective after-tax cost. For the full list of deductible expenses, see [Rental Property Tax Deductions Ontario](/blog/rental-property-tax-deductions-ontario).

**What happens if I miss serving a rent increase notice on time?**
If you don't serve the N1 form at least 90 days before the intended increase date, you cannot implement the increase for that cycle. You'd need to restart the 90-day window for the next cycle. Across four properties with four different tenants and four different increase dates, this is one of the most common and costly administrative errors self-managing landlords make.

**Can a property manager handle LTB filings for me?**
A licensed paralegal or lawyer files LTB applications. Many property managers have a working relationship with a paralegal and will coordinate the process on your behalf — but the legal representation must come from someone licensed under the Law Society of Ontario. When comparing property managers, ask whether paralegal coordination is included or charged separately.

**What software should I use to manage multiple rental properties in Ontario?**
Common options used by Ontario landlords: Buildium and AppFolio for full property management suites, TenantCloud for a lower-cost alternative, and Rent Manager for larger portfolios. All support Canadian rent schedules and lease tracking. For one to three properties with tight budgets, a well-maintained Google Sheets setup works — the discipline matters more than the tool.

**Can I self-manage and still grow my portfolio?**
Yes, but it requires deliberate system-building before you're overwhelmed — not after. Landlords who scale successfully while self-managing typically build their systems at two properties, when they still have capacity. Waiting until four or five properties to build systems means building them in the middle of a crisis.

**Do all my properties need to be in the same city for a property manager to take them on?**
Most property managers focus on a geographic area — in London, that typically covers Middlesex County, Elgin County, and Strathroy. Properties within that range can usually be managed under one agreement. Properties in a different region (Hamilton, Ottawa, Toronto) would typically require a separate local manager.
