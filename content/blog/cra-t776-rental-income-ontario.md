Every Ontario landlord who earns rental income files a T776 — Statement of Real Estate Rentals — with their personal tax return each year. It is not optional, and it is not complicated once you understand what goes where. But most landlords file it without fully understanding it, which means they miss deductions, make errors that attract CRA review, or calculate the wrong net income.

This guide walks through the T776 step by step: what it is, what belongs on it, the deductions you are entitled to claim, the ones you are not, and the mistakes that cost Ontario landlords real money every spring.

---

## What Is the T776?

The T776 is a CRA form called the Statement of Real Estate Rentals. You attach it to your T1 personal income tax return (the form everyone files annually). It is how you report rental income and claim rental expenses against that income.

You file one T776 per property. If you own two rental properties, you file two T776 forms. If you co-own a property with someone else, each owner files their own T776 for their ownership share.

The T776 calculates your net rental income or loss. That net figure flows into line 12600 of your T1 return and is added to your other income (employment, business, investment) for the year. If you have a rental loss, it generally offsets other income, which reduces your total tax owing.

---

## Who Has to File the T776?

You file a T776 if:

- You rented out a property and received rent at any point during the year
- You offered a property for rent (even if you had a vacancy period with no rent collected)
- You co-own a rental property and received any share of the income

You do not file a T776 for:
- A principal residence you lived in yourself (unless you rented out a room or portion)
- A property you sold mid-year with no rental period in that tax year
- Short-term rental income reported differently through a business structure

If you rented out part of your home — a basement apartment while living upstairs, for example — you fill out the T776 for the rental portion only, not the whole property.

## Part 1: Property and Rental Income

The top section of the T776 collects basic property information: address, ownership percentage if co-owned, and the number of units in the building.

Then you report gross rental income. This is the total rent you received during the year — not what you were owed, but what you actually collected. If a tenant owed three months' rent and paid zero, those three months do not appear as income. Ontario landlords who went through an LTB proceeding for rent arrears should be careful here: unpaid rent is not income. You report what arrived in your account.

**Include in gross rental income:**
- Monthly rent from all tenants
- Last month's rent deposit received for the first time (you report it in the year you receive it, not the year it is applied)
- Pet fees, parking, laundry income, and other amounts tenants pay under the lease
- Short-term rental income (Airbnb, VRBO) if reported on this form

**Do not include:**
- Last month's rent deposit you are holding from a prior year (already reported)
- Damage deposits (Ontario landlords cannot legally collect these — but if they exist, they are not income until applied)
- HST, if any (long-term residential rent is HST-exempt under ETA Schedule V, Part I, s.6 — see [HST on rental properties in Ontario](/blog/hst-residential-rental-property-ontario))

## Part 2: Rental Expenses

This is where most of the value is. The T776 lists every category of expense you can deduct against your rental income. Each category has its own line. Here is what each one covers.

### Advertising (Line 8520)

Costs to find tenants. This includes:
- Listing fees on Kijiji, Zumper, Rentals.ca, Facebook Marketplace
- Signs and flyers
- Photographs for your listing
- Fees paid to a tenant placement service

If you paid a property management company a leasing fee or placement fee, it goes here. If you pay ongoing monthly management fees, those go on the management fee line instead.

### Insurance (Line 8690)

Your landlord property insurance premium for the year. This is deductible. Tenant insurance is the tenant's cost, not yours.

If your policy covers both a rental unit and your own residence in the same building, you deduct only the portion attributable to the rental unit.

### Interest (Line 8710)

Mortgage interest on the rental property is deductible — not the full mortgage payment, only the interest portion. Your lender provides an annual statement showing principal and interest separately. Use the interest figure.

If you have a home equity line of credit (HELOC) you used to fund rental property improvements, the interest on funds used for the rental property is deductible.

### Office expenses (Line 8810)

Paper, ink, envelopes, and other minor supplies you use to manage the rental. This is usually a small number.

### Professional fees (Line 8860)

This is an important one that many landlords miss. It includes:

- Accountant or tax preparer fees for preparing the T776 and T1
- Paralegal fees for LTB proceedings related to your rental property
- Legal fees for lease drafting, eviction work, or collections
- Property management consulting fees

LTB filing fees and paralegal costs from an eviction proceeding are deductible here. If you went through an [L1 application](/blog/l1-application-ontario-landlord-guide) or [L2 application](/blog/l2-application-ontario-landlord-guide) last year, those professional fees reduce your taxable income.

### Management and administration fees (Line 8871)

If you use a property management company, their monthly management fees go here. For landlords in London using a full-service PM company at 8–10% of monthly rent on a $2,100/month unit, this is approximately $2,016–$2,520 per year — a meaningful deduction.

### Maintenance and repairs (Line 8960)

Costs to maintain the property in its current condition. This category is frequently confused with capital expenditures (improvements), which are treated differently.

**Deductible maintenance and repairs include:**
- Plumbing and electrical repairs
- HVAC servicing and filter replacement
- Roof patching (but not full replacement — see below)
- Appliance repairs
- Pest control treatments
- Drywall patching and painting between tenants
- Snow removal and landscaping
- Furnace cleaning and tune-up
- Window caulking and weatherstripping

**Not deductible as repairs (capital expenditures instead):**
- Full roof replacement
- New furnace or central air conditioner
- Full kitchen renovation
- New windows throughout the building
- New flooring throughout
- Structural additions

Capital expenditures are added to the adjusted cost base of the property (affecting capital gains on eventual sale) and potentially depreciated through capital cost allowance (CCA) — a separate CCA schedule that is filed alongside the T776. See below for how CCA works.

### Motor vehicle expenses (Line 9281)

You can deduct the cost of driving to and from your rental property for management purposes — inspections, repairs, tenant meetings, showing the unit to prospective tenants. You cannot deduct regular commuting.

CRA requires a mileage log. Without it, the deduction does not survive a review. The log should show date, destination, purpose, and kilometres for each trip.

### Other expenses (Line 9270)

Miscellaneous costs that do not fit other categories. This includes:

- City of London Rental Housing Licence fees — the [annual renewal](/blog/city-of-london-rental-unit-licence-renewal) is fully deductible here
- Property standards inspection fees
- Safety certificate fees (electrical, gas, TSSA)
- Lease-breaking penalties you paid
- LTB application filing fees ($170 online for L1 — deductible)

### Property taxes (Line 9180)

The full annual property tax bill for the rental property. If you own a property that is partly personal residence and partly rental (a duplex where you occupy one unit), you deduct only the portion attributable to the rental unit.

### Salaries, wages, and benefits (Line 9060)

If you pay someone to manage the property — a superintendent, cleaner, or handyman on a payroll basis — those wages are deductible. This applies to employees, not independent contractors (who would go on the management fee or maintenance line instead).

### Travel (Line 9200)

If you travel to visit the rental property and the property is not in the same city where you live — for out-of-province landlords, for example — travel costs can be deductible. This is strictly for out-of-town properties where travel to inspect or manage is genuinely required. Local landlords in London, St. Thomas, or Strathroy use the motor vehicle expense line instead.

### Utilities (Line 9220)

If you pay utilities for the rental unit — heat, electricity, water — you deduct them here. If the lease makes the tenant responsible for utilities (which is standard in London for single-unit rentals), you have no utility costs to deduct.

For small multi-unit buildings where common areas have landlord-paid utilities, deduct that portion.

## Personal vs. Rental Use

If you own a property that you use personally for part of the year — a cottage rented out seasonally, or a condo you live in for part of the year and rent out for the rest — you must prorate all expenses by the percentage of time (or space) used for rental purposes.

CRA looks at two factors: time and space. If you rented the cottage for 8 of 12 months, your deductible expenses are approximately 67% of total costs. If you live in one of two units in a duplex, your deductible expenses are approximately 50%.

---

## Net Income or Loss

After totalling all rental expenses, you subtract them from gross rental income. The result is your net rental income (positive) or net rental loss (negative).

Net rental income is added to your total income. Net rental loss reduces your total income, which reduces tax owing. Many Ontario landlords — particularly those with recent purchases and high mortgage interest — run small annual losses. This is a normal tax outcome, not a problem.

---

## Capital Cost Allowance (CCA)

CCA is depreciation on the building itself. It is optional — you are never required to claim it. And claiming it creates a risk on sale called recapture, where the CCA you claimed is added back to income when the property is sold.

The basic rule: buildings (Class 1 or Class 3) depreciate at 4% per year of declining balance, subject to the half-year rule in year of acquisition. You claim it on a separate CCA schedule attached to the T776.

Most small Ontario landlords choose not to claim CCA because:
1. The recapture on sale is fully taxable, often making the deferrals not worthwhile
2. Many properties are worth more than their original cost, making the recapture risk real
3. Rental losses from other deductions are usually sufficient to reduce taxes adequately

If you are considering claiming CCA, speak with an accountant first. The capital cost allowance rules are covered in more depth in a separate guide — see [capital cost allowance for rental property Ontario](/blog/capital-cost-allowance-rental-property-ontario) when it is available.

## Common T776 Mistakes Ontario Landlords Make

**1. Reporting last month's rent deposit as income when it's held, not applied**
You report the deposit in the year you receive it, not the year the final month's rent is applied. If you collected the deposit in 2023 and the tenant moved out in 2026, it was income in 2023 — not 2026.

**2. Deducting capital expenditures as repairs**
A new roof is not a repair. A new furnace is not a repair. Treat them as capital additions to the property's cost base and potentially claim CCA.

**3. Missing the professional fees deduction**
LTB paralegal fees, accountant fees, and legal costs related to the rental property are all deductible. Many landlords forget to include the year they went through an eviction.

**4. No mileage log for motor vehicle expenses**
CRA will ask for a log. Without it, the deduction disappears on audit.

**5. Missing the City of London Rental Housing Licence fee**
If you own a rental in London, the annual [RHL licence fee](/blog/city-of-london-rental-unit-licence) is a deductible other expense. Many landlords never claim it.

**6. Forgetting to deduct management fees properly**
One-time tenant placement fees (advertising/leasing) go on Line 8520. Ongoing monthly management fees go on Line 8871. Using the wrong line is not a catastrophic error, but it creates inconsistency that can flag a review.

**7. Filing one T776 for two properties**
CRA expects one T776 per property. Combining two properties on one form makes it impossible to separate the income and expenses accurately and complicates any future review.

**8. Not keeping receipts until the limitation period passes**
CRA can reassess returns for three years from the date of the original notice of assessment (longer for certain misrepresentations). Keep all rental expense receipts for at least six years.

## What Records to Keep

Good records are the foundation of a clean T776 and your protection in a CRA review. Maintain:

- All rent receipts and rent payment records (the same system you use for [landlord record keeping](/blog/landlord-record-keeping-ontario) works here)
- All expense receipts, organized by T776 category
- Annual mortgage interest statement from your lender
- Property tax bill and payment confirmation
- Insurance policy and premium receipt
- Any invoices for professional fees, management fees, repairs, or maintenance
- Mileage log if claiming vehicle expenses
- Proof of LTB filing fees (screenshots from the LTB online portal)

The CRA requires you to keep records for at least six years from the end of the tax year to which they relate. Store them digitally — photos of receipts in a folder organized by tax year and expense category is sufficient and survives better than paper.

## London, St. Thomas, and Strathroy: Local Expenses to Track

Ontario landlords in this region have several location-specific deductible expenses:

**City of London:**
- Rental Housing Licence application fee ($275–$350 for a new licence)
- Annual licence renewal fee
- Property standards inspection fee (if applicable)
- Second Unit Registration fee (if applicable)

**St. Thomas:**
- Property standards inspection fee (if any)
- Building permit fees for renovations

**Strathroy-Caradoc:**
- Building permit fees for any permitted work
- Property standards compliance costs

All of these are deductible as "other expenses" on Line 9270 of the T776.

## When Is the T776 Due?

The T776 is filed with your T1 personal tax return. The standard filing deadline is **April 30** each year for the prior calendar year (so your 2026 rental income is reported in a T776 filed by April 30, 2027).

If you or your spouse are self-employed, the deadline extends to June 15 — but any tax owing is still due April 30.

For most Ontario landlords, the T1 deadline is April 30. File before that date to avoid late-filing penalties and interest on any balance owing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do I have to file the T776 even if I had a loss?**
Yes. If you owned and operated a rental property at any point during the year, you must file the T776 even if expenses exceeded income. A rental loss reduces your other taxable income.

**Can I deduct the mortgage principal?**
No. Only the interest portion of your mortgage payment is deductible, not the principal repayment.

**What if my tenant paid rent in cash?**
Cash rent is still rental income. Report it. CRA uses bank deposits, property tax records, and other signals to identify unreported rental income. The risk of underreporting significantly exceeds the tax saved.

**Can I claim rental expenses from a vacant property?**
Yes, as long as you are actively attempting to rent the property. Keep records showing the property was available for rent — listings, showing notes, advertising costs. Pure vacancy with no effort to rent does not support deductions.

**My tenant covered their own utilities. Can I still deduct utilities?**
Only if you paid them. If your tenant pays utilities directly under the lease, you have nothing to deduct on that line.

**What if I forgot to claim an expense in a prior year?**
You can file a T1 adjustment (T1ADJ form) for prior years. CRA allows adjustments going back 10 years. If you have missed significant deductions in past returns, it may be worth reviewing with an accountant.

**Is rental income from an Airbnb treated the same way?**
Not exactly. Short-term rentals (under 30 days) may attract HST obligations that long-term residential rent does not. The income is still reported — but the form used and the HST implications differ. See [HST on rental properties in Ontario](/blog/hst-residential-rental-property-ontario) for details.

**Do I need a separate T776 for each province if I own rental properties in multiple provinces?**
Yes. Each province's property goes on its own T776. The rental income from each flows to your T1 based on where the property is located.

## The Bottom Line

The T776 is not complicated — but it has enough moving parts that landlords who file without thinking carefully about it leave money on the table every year. The most common outcome is missed deductions: professional fees from last year's LTB proceeding, the City of London licence fee, management fees filed on the wrong line, motor vehicle expenses with no mileage log to back them up.

Good record keeping throughout the year — receipts organized by T776 category, mortgage interest statements saved, mileage tracked — makes the form straightforward to file and your numbers defensible if CRA ever asks questions.

If your rental portfolio is growing, or your first year of rental income feels complex, working with an accountant who handles rental properties is worth the professional fee — and that fee is itself deductible on next year's T776.
