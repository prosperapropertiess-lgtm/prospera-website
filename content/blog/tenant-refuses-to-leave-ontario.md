Your tenant has been given valid notice. The deadline passed. They haven't moved. They aren't returning calls. They are simply still there.

This is one of the most stressful situations a landlord can face — and it is more common than most people realize. Ontario's eviction process is longer and more procedural than most landlords expect, and knowing exactly what comes next is what separates landlords who resolve this cleanly from landlords who make costly mistakes.

Here is what you can do, what you cannot do, and what the process looks like from notice to empty unit.

---

## Why Tenants Can Legally Stay Past a Notice Date

This surprises many first-time landlords: in Ontario, a valid notice to terminate does not automatically end the tenancy. Under the Residential Tenancies Act, a notice only gives the landlord the right to apply to the Landlord and Tenant Board (LTB). The tenant does not have to leave just because they received a notice.

The only way to legally force a tenant to vacate — if they refuse to leave voluntarily — is through an LTB eviction order enforced by the Sheriff.

That is the process. There are no shortcuts.

---

## Step 1: Confirm Your Notice Was Valid

Before you file anything, make sure your notice was properly served. A defective notice restarts the clock.

**Common issues that invalidate a notice:**
- Wrong termination date (must land on the last day of a rental period for most N-form notices)
- Served by an invalid method (see [how to serve notice in Ontario](/blog/how-to-serve-notice-ontario-landlords))
- Incorrect form used for the grounds
- Notice served too close to the termination date (insufficient notice period)

If you used an N4 for unpaid rent, the termination date must be at least 14 days after service. If you used an N8 (end of fixed term for consistent late payment), it must land at the end of a rental period with 60 days' notice.

If the notice has an error, file a new one before applying to the LTB. Applying on a defective notice wastes filing fees and delays your case.

---

## Step 2: File the Right LTB Application

Once the termination date in your notice passes without the tenant leaving, you can file an application with the LTB.

**Which application you file depends on what notice you used:**

| Situation | Notice | LTB Application |
|---|---|---|
| Unpaid rent | N4 | [L1 application](/blog/l1-application-ontario-landlord-guide) |
| Persistent behaviour problems | N5, N6, N7 | [L2 application](/blog/l2-application-ontario-landlord-guide) |
| End of fixed term / persistent late rent | N8 | L2 application |
| Landlord or family moving in | N12 | L2 application |
| Major repairs / renovations | N13 | L2 application |
| Tenant agreed to leave (N11) | N11 | [L3 application](/blog/l3-application-ontario-landlord-guide) |
| Tenant refused to pay after agreement | Existing order | [L4 application](/blog/l4-application-ontario-landlord-guide) |

For non-payment cases, you file the L1 application online through the LTB portal at [tribunalsontario.ca/ltb](https://tribunalsontario.ca/ltb). The filing fee for an L1 is $201.

**Important for N12 cases:** If your tenant received an N12 (own use) but refuses to leave, you will need to apply for an eviction order. The tenant also has the right to dispute the N12 at the LTB, so a hearing may be scheduled. See [N12 notice guide](/blog/n12-notice-ontario) for the specific process.

---

## Step 3: Wait for the Hearing

After you file, the LTB will schedule a hearing. In the London / Middlesex area, L1 hearings currently take approximately 8–14 weeks from filing date to hearing date. L2 cases take slightly longer due to the broader range of grounds.

During this period, the tenant can remain in the unit. This is frustrating, but it is the law.

**If you have an L1 for unpaid rent:** Rent continues to accrue during the waiting period. Keep records of every missed payment. At the hearing, you can claim the full outstanding arrears up to the date of the hearing — not just the amount on the original N4.

**If you have an L2 for behaviour or termination grounds:** Document every incident with dates, times, photos, and any communication records. See [how to prepare for an LTB hearing](/blog/ltb-hearing-preparation-ontario-landlords) for a full list of what to bring.

---

## Step 4: Get the Eviction Order

If the LTB rules in your favour, it will issue an order requiring the tenant to pay arrears or vacate by a specific date — or both.

**What if the tenant ignores the order?**

An LTB order is not self-enforcing. If the tenant does not comply by the date in the order, you must file a request for a Sheriff's enforcement warrant. You cannot remove the tenant yourself.

The Sheriff enforcement process works like this:

1. Obtain the LTB order
2. Wait for the compliance date to pass (tenant fails to comply)
3. File a Court Enforcement Office request (now done through the LTB portal or in person) — there is a fee of approximately $450–$560 for Sheriff enforcement in Middlesex County
4. The Sheriff will schedule an enforcement visit, typically 3–6 weeks after your request
5. On enforcement day, the Sheriff attends the unit with you present and supervises the tenant's exit
6. If the tenant still refuses to leave at that point, the Sheriff has authority to remove them

The [enforcing an eviction order in Ontario](/blog/enforcing-eviction-order-ontario-sheriff) guide covers the full Sheriff process including timelines, what to bring, and what happens if the tenant re-enters after removal.

## What If the Tenant Has an LTB Order Issued Against Them and Still Won't Leave?

This happens. The order is issued, the compliance date passes, and the tenant is still there.

Do not:
- Change the locks
- Remove any of the tenant's belongings
- Shut off utilities
- Threaten or physically confront the tenant
- Refuse to perform maintenance during this period

All of these actions are illegal under the RTA. A tenant who is served with an eviction order still retains all RTA rights until the Sheriff physically removes them. Interfering with the tenant before that point exposes you to significant liability — including rent abatement orders and damage awards at the LTB.

Your only legal path forward is Sheriff enforcement.

---

## What If the Tenant Left Belongings Behind?

Once the Sheriff has carried out the enforcement and the tenant is out, any belongings left in the unit are your problem to manage carefully.

Under the RTA:
- You cannot simply throw away or sell a former tenant's property
- You must store the items for a reasonable period
- You should document everything with photos before touching anything
- Contact the former tenant in writing to retrieve their belongings, with a clear deadline

The practical risk here is small if you document properly. The legal risk of discarding belongings without documentation can be a civil claim from the former tenant.

## Situations Where This Gets More Complicated

### Tenant Claims the Notice Was Served Incorrectly

If the tenant disputes the notice at the LTB — claiming it was left at the wrong address, served by an unauthorized method, or received late — the hearing will address this before anything else.

This is why proper service matters from the beginning. Under RTA s.191, [valid service methods](/blog/how-to-serve-notice-ontario-landlords) include hand-delivery to the tenant personally, dropping through the mail slot at the rental address, or sending by mail (with a 5-day deemed receipt addition to the notice period).

### Tenant Files a T-Application as a Defence

Tenants sometimes file T2 (interference with reasonable enjoyment), T6 (maintenance), or other applications as a tactic or as a genuine dispute. This can complicate your hearing.

This does not stop the eviction process, but it means multiple applications will be heard together. Address any maintenance or interference issues proactively, and keep documentation of your compliance.

### Tenant Claims Landlord Is Retaliating

If you served notice shortly after the tenant raised a maintenance complaint or exercised an RTA right, the tenant may argue the notice is retaliatory. The LTB will look at timing and context.

This is not a basis to avoid filing, but it does mean you need to be prepared to demonstrate legitimate grounds. See the guide on [landlord retaliation Ontario](/blog/landlord-retaliation-ontario-rta-section-97) for the specific RTA rules.

### The Tenant Has Stopped Paying Rent But Hasn't Left

This is an L1 situation. See [what to do if your tenant stops paying rent in Ontario](/blog/what-to-do-if-tenant-stops-paying-rent-ontario) for the full process.

## The Full Timeline: What to Expect

Here is a realistic timeline for a standard non-payment eviction in London / SW Ontario, starting from the day rent is missed:

| Stage | Approximate Time |
|---|---|
| Serve N4, wait for 14-day cure period | Days 1–14 |
| Tenant doesn't pay, file L1 with LTB | Day 15–21 |
| LTB schedules hearing | 8–14 weeks from filing |
| Hearing, LTB issues order | Same day or within 2 weeks |
| Compliance date in order passes | Typically 11 days after order |
| File for Sheriff enforcement | Day of non-compliance |
| Sheriff enforcement visit | 3–6 weeks after request |
| **Total: Missed rent to vacant unit** | **4–6 months** |

For behaviour-based evictions (N5/N6/N8/N12), timelines vary. Some require a voidable period before filing; some require two notices before an application can be made. See the specific guide for each notice type.

## 7 Common Mistakes When a Tenant Refuses to Leave

**1. Changing the locks or removing belongings.** This is called a self-help eviction. It is illegal under RTA s.33 and s.34, can result in an LTB order against you, and will delay your legitimate eviction case.

**2. Applying to the LTB without first serving a valid notice.** The notice is a prerequisite. You cannot skip it.

**3. Accepting a partial rent payment after the N4 termination date.** Once you accept rent after the termination date on an N4, the notice is voided and you must serve a new one.

**4. Missing the LTB filing deadline.** For some applications (like L3 when a tenant has given an N9), there is a 30-day filing window. For L1 and L2 applications, there is no strict deadline, but delay increases arrears and tenant entrenchment.

**5. Not keeping records during the process.** The hearing will require proof of service, payment history, and documentation of grounds. Missing records can get a case dismissed or ordered in the tenant's favour.

**6. Assuming the LTB order enforces itself.** It does not. If the tenant doesn't comply, you must file for Sheriff enforcement — it does not happen automatically.

**7. Confronting or pressuring the tenant.** Even if your frustration is completely understandable, any behaviour that could be characterised as harassment, intimidation, or interference with the tenant's reasonable enjoyment will hurt your LTB case and could result in a T2 order against you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I call the police if my tenant refuses to leave?**
Police generally will not remove a tenant from a rental unit — they treat this as a civil matter, not a criminal one, unless there is a threat of violence or a specific criminal offence. Your path is through the LTB and the Sheriff.

**What if I need the unit for a family member and the tenant won't leave?**
If you served an N12, the process is the same: file an L2 application after the termination date passes. The tenant has the right to dispute the N12 at the hearing. If successful, the LTB will issue an eviction order and the tenant will be removed by the Sheriff on the enforcement date.

**Can I evict a tenant faster if they haven't paid in months?**
No. The process is the same regardless of how much rent is owed. What changes is the amount you can claim in arrears — the full outstanding balance can be included in your L1 application, and arrears continue to accrue until the LTB hearing.

**What if the tenant leaves some belongings but I'm not sure they've actually moved out?**
Document the state of the unit with photos. Send written notice to the tenant asking them to confirm their intention and retrieve any remaining belongings by a specific date. Do not re-rent the unit until you are certain the tenancy has ended and you have documented the move-out inspection. See the [move-in and move-out inspection guide](/blog/move-in-move-out-inspection-ontario).

**Can my tenant be evicted more quickly if they're causing problems for other tenants?**
If you have grounds under s.22 (substantial interference with reasonable enjoyment of other tenants), you can file an N5. The N5 gives the tenant a 7-day void period to correct the behaviour. If they don't, you can file an L2 without serving a second N5. This doesn't necessarily speed up the LTB timeline, but it establishes the basis for eviction without the behaviour pattern requirement.

**Is there a faster process if the tenant agreed to leave but changed their mind?**
If you have a signed N11 (Agreement to End a Tenancy), you can file an L3 application, which is a no-hearing eviction pathway — the LTB can issue an order without a full hearing in many cases. See [L3 application guide](/blog/l3-application-ontario-landlord-guide) for how this works.

**What if I find out the tenant has sublet without my permission?**
Unauthorized subletting can be grounds for an N5 notice (unauthorized subletting is listed as a ground in RTA s.97). See [subletting and assignment in Ontario](/blog/subletting-assignment-ontario) for the specific rules.

**Can a tenant stop an eviction order after the LTB issues it?**
Yes. A tenant can request a review of an LTB order under RTA s.209 within 30 days of the order. This can delay enforcement. See [LTB review order Ontario](/blog/ltb-review-order-ontario) for how the review process works and what grounds the LTB accepts.
