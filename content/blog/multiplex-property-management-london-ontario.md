If you own a single rental unit in London, Ontario, you manage one tenant relationship, one lease renewal cycle, one maintenance call at a time. That's manageable for most people — time-consuming, but manageable.

A duplex, triplex, or fourplex is a different job. Not just more of the same — structurally different. Multiple tenants under the same roof, overlapping maintenance obligations, coordinated rent increase cycles, three or four separate Rental Housing Licences, and an LTB compliance load that multiplies with each door.

This guide covers what actually changes when you move from one unit to multiple, what operations look like for a London multiplex owner, and when professional management stops being optional.

---

## Why Multiplex Operations Don't Scale Like Single Units

The most common mistake London multiplex owners make is treating each unit as a separate single-unit property that happens to share a structure. That's the wrong frame.

A multiplex is one asset with multiple revenue streams. Maintenance decisions in one unit affect the building as a whole. A structural issue in the basement affects every tenant. A pest issue in one unit spreads to others if you don't respond within days. A heating failure in winter affects all tenants simultaneously and triggers parallel maintenance obligations under RTA s.20 for each one.

**What stays the same regardless of unit count:**

- Every tenancy is governed by the Residential Tenancies Act
- Every rent increase requires a completed N1 and 90 days' written notice
- Every late rent situation requires an N4 before an L1 application
- The LTB hearing timeline in London (8–14 weeks, London Regional LTB Office) is the same whether you have one unit or four
- The City of London Rental Housing Licence requirement applies to each unit individually

**What changes:**

- Maintenance events become simultaneous rather than sequential
- Compliance cycles (N1s, deposit interest, licence renewals) run in parallel across units
- Vacancy gaps in multiple units compound the financial impact
- Noise and interference complaints between tenants introduce landlord liability under RTA s.22
- Record-keeping requirements multiply by unit count
- Insurance and liability exposure covers a larger structure with more occupants

## The City of London Rental Housing Licence — One Per Unit

This is the most common London-specific error multiplex owners make. The [City of London Rental Housing Licence](/blog/city-of-london-rental-unit-licence) applies per rental unit, not per property.

If you own a triplex in London, you need three separate licences. Each licence has its own application, its own annual renewal, and its own compliance history. If the city conducts a property standards inspection and finds violations, those violations can block renewal for the affected unit — not necessarily the whole building.

**Practical implications for multiplex owners:**

- Budget $275–$350 per unit per year in licensing fees for a first-year application
- Track each unit's renewal date separately — they may not align
- Keep a maintenance log for each unit to support inspection readiness
- Ensure the address on file for each licence is current — renewal notices go by mail

If you manage a triplex with units at first, second, and third floor, you're running three separate licensing obligations year-round. Missing one renewal is an offence under the city's Rental Housing By-law, and operating without a licence can complicate LTB proceedings if a tenant raises it at a hearing.

For detailed compliance steps, see [City of London Rental Housing Licence renewal](/blog/city-of-london-rental-unit-licence-renewal).

## Rent Increase Management Across Multiple Units

Each tenancy has its own 12-month cycle. In a duplex, you might have two tenants who moved in six months apart — which means two separate N1 notices with different effective dates, different calculation amounts, and different deemed-receipt windows.

In a fourplex, you could be managing four separate increase cycles across a calendar year.

**The compliance risk:** Most landlords who self-manage a multiplex fall behind on this. They apply increases inconsistently, miss the 90-day window for one unit, or serve a notice to the wrong address. Under RTA s.136, a rent increase is void if the notice is defective — which means you lose the revenue for that cycle and need to restart the 90-day clock.

For rent-controlled units (first occupied before November 15, 2018), that void increase also means you cannot apply it retroactively — the opportunity is gone until the next cycle.

**A working system looks like this:**

1. Track each unit's lease start date in a spreadsheet or property management software
2. Set a recurring calendar reminder 120 days before the next eligible increase date (90 days' notice + 30 days' buffer)
3. Prepare and serve the N1 for each unit on its own schedule
4. File a copy with the date served and method of service

For how to calculate increases correctly, see [how to increase rent in Ontario step by step](/blog/how-to-increase-rent-ontario-step-by-step).

## Last-Month's Rent Deposit Interest — Four Times the Obligation

Under RTA s.106(6), landlords must pay annual interest on the last month's rent deposit at the rent increase guideline rate. For 2026, the guideline is 2.5%.

For a single-unit landlord, this is one annual calculation. For a fourplex owner, it's four — one per unit, with different deposit amounts and different deposit dates. The accumulated interest can be paid as a rent credit or a cash payment.

Most multiplex owners don't do this at all. The consequence is a T1 application from a tenant, which can recover unpaid interest going back one year. With four units and $1,900–$2,200 deposits each, the accrued liability across a multiplex can reach $600–$800 per year if ignored.

For the full calculation and payment mechanics, see [last month's rent deposit interest in Ontario](/blog/last-month-rent-interest-ontario).

## Noise and Interference Complaints Between Tenants

A single-unit landlord rarely deals with noise complaints about their tenant — that's a neighbour issue or a bylaw call. A multiplex landlord has a legal obligation to address noise and interference between tenants under RTA s.22 (quiet enjoyment) and s.26 (superintendent obligations where applicable).

If tenant A in Unit 1 is making excessive noise that substantially interferes with tenant B in Unit 2, tenant B has a right to file a T2 application against the landlord — not just against their neighbour. The LTB can award rent abatement, compensation, and an order requiring the landlord to take corrective action.

**What landlords must do when an inter-tenant noise complaint is received:**

1. Document the complaint in writing with date and details
2. Respond to the complaining tenant in writing within 24–48 hours
3. Serve the offending tenant a written warning (keep a copy)
4. If the behaviour continues, consider an N5 notice for interference with reasonable enjoyment
5. Keep a log of all steps taken

Silence is not a defence at the LTB. If you ignore the complaint and the complaining tenant files a T2, the landlord's inaction itself becomes the basis of the application.

For the full rights and obligations under s.22, see [RTA s.22 interference with reasonable enjoyment](/blog/rta-section-22-interference-reasonable-enjoyment-ontario).

## Maintenance Coordination in a Multiplex

Single-unit maintenance is linear: one call, one repair, one invoice. Multiplex maintenance requires coordination across multiple occupied units.

**Common multiplex maintenance scenarios:**

- Roof or foundation work that requires access to all units simultaneously (24-hour notice required for each)
- Shared heating system failure that affects all tenants (s.20 obligation, emergency timeline — no heat in winter = 24-hour response requirement)
- Plumbing in a shared stack that requires access to units above and below
- Pest control that must be conducted in all units at once (a single treated unit will just see reinfestation from the untreated ones)
- Exterior work that affects parking, access, or outdoor areas

Each entry into an occupied unit requires 24-hour written notice to that specific tenant under RTA s.27. In a triplex, coordinating a single maintenance visit with three separate notice requirements adds time and administrative load.

**City of London Property Standards intersection:** If the city issues an Order to Comply for the building, the order typically covers the whole property — but your compliance obligation runs separately for each occupied unit. An unresolved standards order can block your licence renewal for affected units.

For maintenance documentation requirements, see [RTA s.20 maintenance obligations Ontario](/blog/rta-section-20-maintenance-obligations-ontario) and [landlord maintenance responsibilities Ontario](/blog/landlord-maintenance-responsibilities-ontario).

## Vacancy Coordination and Turnover

When one unit in a multiplex becomes vacant, the other units are occupied. Turnover work — cleaning, repairs, fresh paint, appliance checks — must be coordinated without disturbing existing tenants.

**What changes at the turnover stage:**

- Paint fumes and construction noise affect occupied units; notify existing tenants in advance
- Contractors need to access shared areas (hallways, basement, exterior) during turnover
- If you're upgrading the vacant unit to attract a higher rent, existing tenants may notice and ask questions about their own units or future rent increases
- Shared utilities (if any) may need to be managed during the work period

Timing vacancy turnover well is more valuable in a multiplex than in a single unit. An extended vacancy in one unit reduces your overall gross yield by a larger proportion. For a triplex at $2,000/month per unit, two weeks of vacancy is worth $1,000 in lost income — which exceeds a typical professional placement fee.

## Insurance and Liability for Multi-Unit Buildings

A standard rental property insurance policy for a single unit covers one dwelling. A multiplex requires a policy specifically structured for multi-unit residential properties.

The key differences:

- **Liability coverage** must cover injuries to any occupant or visitor in any unit or in common areas — a staircase injury, a slip on shared ice, a fall in the shared laundry room
- **Loss of rental income** coverage should reflect the total gross rent across all units, not just one
- **Property damage coverage** must cover the full replacement cost of the building, not just one unit's contents
- **Vacancy clauses** apply differently — if one unit is vacant during a renovation, your insurer needs to know; failing to notify can void a claim for damage to the vacant unit

If the building includes a basement apartment that was added after the original construction, confirm your insurer knows it exists and that it's registered (City of London Second Unit Registration). An unregistered suite that isn't reflected in your policy is a coverage gap.

For more on rental property insurance obligations, see [rental property insurance Ontario](/blog/rental-property-insurance-ontario).

## Record-Keeping for a London Multiplex

The [landlord record-keeping obligations under the RTA](/blog/landlord-record-keeping-ontario) are the same regardless of how many units you own. But the volume of records grows directly with unit count.

For a fourplex, you're maintaining:

- Four lease agreements (original + any amendments)
- Four last-month's rent deposit records with interest calculations
- Four move-in inspection reports with photos
- Four sets of maintenance logs
- Four N1 service records per annual rent increase cycle
- Four separate City of London Rental Housing Licence files
- Four Certificate of Service records for every notice served
- Income and expense records for each unit for CRA T776 purposes

If you're ever at the LTB — for any unit — you need to produce records for that unit promptly. Incomplete records are one of the most common reasons landlord applications are dismissed or reduced at a hearing.

For tax record-keeping specific to multi-unit properties, see [CRA T776 rental income Ontario](/blog/cra-t776-rental-income-ontario).

## When Self-Management Stops Working for a London Multiplex

Most landlords who start self-managing a duplex can keep up with it, especially if they're local and have a good relationship with their tenants. The breaking points tend to arrive at three or four units.

**Signals that the multiplex is past the self-managing threshold:**

- Rent increase cycles are being missed or applied inconsistently
- Licence renewals are coming due and you're not sure of the dates
- You're responding to maintenance calls reactively rather than on a scheduled inspection basis
- Deposit interest has never been calculated or paid
- Tenant complaints are going unacknowledged for more than 48 hours
- You've had an LTB application where missing documentation hurt your case
- You have a job, and the building is consuming weekend and evening time you can't afford

**What professional management adds for a multiplex owner:**

- All four compliance cycles (N1 timing, deposit interest, licence renewals, move-out inspections) tracked and executed on schedule
- A single contact point for all four tenants for maintenance calls, reducing your personal involvement to approvals and reports
- Coordinated maintenance vendors who already know the property
- Vacancy filling with a placement process that covers City of London Human Rights Code requirements and the Standard Lease
- LTB application handling if a tenancy goes badly, with documentation already in place
- A single monthly statement showing gross rent, expenses, and net income across all units

The management fee — typically 8–10% of gross rent in London — is a deductible business expense on your CRA T776. For a triplex at $2,100/month per unit, that's roughly $500–$630/month. The question is whether the operational complexity you're absorbing exceeds that number in time, risk, and missed compliance.

For a detailed cost comparison, see [property manager vs. self-managing Ontario](/blog/property-manager-vs-self-managing-ontario).

## Common Mistakes Multiplex Owners Make

**1. Treating each unit as a standalone property.**
One building, one maintenance system, one record-keeping structure — but four separate tenancy timelines. Both are true simultaneously.

**2. Losing track of rent increase eligibility dates.**
Four different move-in dates means four different eligible increase dates. Spreadsheet or calendar — pick one and use it consistently.

**3. Not paying annual deposit interest.**
Four separate deposits, four annual interest payments at the guideline rate. This obligation accumulates and becomes a T1 liability.

**4. Applying for a single Rental Housing Licence.**
Each unit in London requires its own licence. Applying for one and operating all four puts three units in violation.

**5. Failing to respond to inter-tenant complaints.**
In a multiplex, landlord inaction on noise and interference is a s.22 violation — not just a building management failure.

**6. Not tracking rent-controlled vs. exempt units.**
In a building where some units were first occupied before November 15, 2018 and others after, the guideline cap applies to the older units but not the newer ones. Each unit's control status must be tracked separately.

**7. Using one insurance policy designed for a single-family rental.**
Confirm with your broker that the policy covers a multi-unit residential property with the correct liability and loss-of-rent coverage across all units.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I manage a London triplex myself without professional help?**
Yes — many landlords do. The critical factor is whether your systems are keeping up with the compliance load: N1 timings, deposit interest, licence renewals, maintenance documentation. If any of these are slipping, that's a signal.

**Does each unit in my duplex need its own Rental Housing Licence in London?**
Yes. The City of London licensing program applies per rental unit, not per property address. A duplex requires two licences.

**If I raise rent for one unit in my multiplex, do I have to raise it for all units?**
No. Each tenancy is independent. You can raise rent in one unit and not others, as long as each increase follows the N1 process (90-day written notice, once per 12 months per unit).

**What happens if one tenant in my triplex has a problem with another tenant?**
You have a legal obligation to address it. Ignoring an inter-tenant noise or harassment complaint can expose you to a T2 application from the complaining tenant against you as the landlord.

**Are property management fees for a multiplex deductible on my taxes?**
Yes. Management fees are deductible as a business expense on your CRA T776 Statement of Real Estate Rentals, allocated to the relevant property.

**How does the LTB process work if I have an issue with a tenant in one unit of my multiplex?**
Exactly the same as it would for any single-unit landlord. The LTB process, notice requirements, and timelines are identical. A problem in Unit 2 of a triplex proceeds the same way as a problem in a standalone rental.

**Can I convert a fourplex to a triplex to simplify management?**
Yes, but it requires a building permit and may require zoning approval depending on the neighbourhood. Converting an occupied unit also triggers RTA obligations — you cannot terminate a tenancy for conversion without following the N13 process and providing proper compensation.

**Do I need a separate lease for each unit in my multiplex?**
Yes. Each tenancy is a separate legal relationship under the RTA and requires its own Ontario Standard Lease.
