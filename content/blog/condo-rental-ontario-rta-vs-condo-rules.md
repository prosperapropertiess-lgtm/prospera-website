If you rent out a condo in Ontario, you operate under two separate rule systems at the same time: the **Residential Tenancies Act (RTA)** and your **condominium corporation's rules, bylaws, and declaration**.

Both apply. And they sometimes contradict each other.

When that happens, most landlords — and many condo boards — assume the condo corporation's rules win. That assumption is usually wrong. The RTA is provincial legislation. Condo rules are private contracts. Where they conflict, the RTA takes priority.

This post explains exactly how this plays out: which rules govern what, where the conflicts commonly arise, and how to protect yourself as a condo landlord in Ontario.

---

## The Legal Framework: Two Rule Systems, One Unit

### The Residential Tenancies Act (RTA)

The RTA governs the relationship between landlords and tenants across Ontario. It sets the rules for:

- Rent increases and rent control exemptions
- Entry by the landlord
- Lease terms and renewals
- Eviction grounds and procedures
- Maintenance and repair obligations
- Tenant rights to quiet enjoyment

The RTA applies to **almost every residential rental in Ontario**, including condos. It is provincial law. It cannot be watered down by any private agreement — including a condo corporation's rules.

### Condo Corporation Rules, Bylaws, and Declaration

A condominium corporation creates three types of governing documents:

1. **Declaration** — the founding document, registered on title. It defines ownership, common elements, unit boundaries, and restrictions.
2. **Bylaws** — govern the operations of the corporation. Voting procedures, board composition, assessments.
3. **Rules** — day-to-day conduct. Noise, amenity use, move-in procedures, pet policies.

As a condo owner, you agreed to follow these when you bought the unit. They are binding on you as an owner.

The question is: how much of that obligation transfers to your tenant?

---

## What the Condominium Act Says About Tenants

Section 83 of the **Condominium Act, 1998** requires you, as a landlord-owner, to give your tenant a copy of the condo's declaration, bylaws, and rules **before or on the day the lease starts**.

Your tenant then becomes bound by those rules — but only to the extent those rules don't violate the RTA.

That's the key phrase. The Condominium Act explicitly preserves the tenant's rights under the RTA. A condo rule cannot strip a tenant of rights the RTA gives them.

## Where Condo Rules and the RTA Commonly Conflict

### 1. Pet Policies

This is the most common conflict — and the one that surprises condo landlords the most.

Many condo corporations have a strict **no-pets clause** in their rules or declaration. Some have breed or size restrictions.

Here's the problem: the RTA prohibits no-pet clauses in residential tenancies. Under section 14 of the RTA, a no-pets provision in a lease is void. A landlord cannot evict a tenant for having a pet unless the pet is causing damage, disturbing other residents, or the tenant agreed to no pets in a valid provision elsewhere.

**What happens when a condo rule bans pets but a tenant brings one in?**

This is unresolved territory. The leading case law suggests the RTA protects the tenant's right to have pets as against the landlord. But the condo corporation's rules bind the owner — which is you.

The practical result: the condo corporation can pressure **you** to enforce its no-pet rule. You, in turn, may have limited ability to do so without an eviction process that the LTB may not support.

**What to do:** Be transparent with prospective tenants about the condo's pet rules upfront. Document the rule in the lease. If the condo has a strict no-pet policy, seek legal advice before renting to a tenant with pets.

### 2. Landlord Entry Rights

The RTA sets the rules for when and how a landlord can enter a rented unit:

- 24 hours written notice for most entries
- Entry only between 8 a.m. and 8 p.m.
- Immediate entry allowed in emergencies

A condo corporation's rules cannot override this. If your condo's property management team wants to inspect the unit or access it for common element maintenance, those same rules apply. The corporation must work through you, and you must follow the RTA's notice requirements to your tenant.

**Common scenario:** The condo board wants to inspect every unit for a building-wide plumbing check. They give owners 48 hours notice. That's not enough notice to your tenant. You need to give your tenant 24 hours written notice for each entry — a separate notice to them, even if the board told you as the owner.

For more on entry rights, see our post on [landlord entry rights in Ontario](/blog/landlord-entry-rights-ontario).

### 3. Lease Terms and Restrictions

Condo rules occasionally restrict the duration of leases — for example, a minimum lease term of one year, or restrictions on month-to-month tenancies.

These restrictions bind you as an owner. But once you have a tenant, the RTA governs what happens to that lease.

Under the RTA:
- A fixed-term lease automatically converts to a month-to-month tenancy when it expires, unless the tenant agrees otherwise or leaves
- A landlord cannot force a tenant to sign a new lease at the end of a fixed term

If the condo's rules require leases to be renewed at minimum one year, but the RTA says the tenant can stay month-to-month, the RTA wins. You cannot evict a tenant simply for refusing to sign a new fixed-term lease.

For more on what happens at the end of a lease, see our guide on [lease renewals in Ontario](/blog/lease-renewal-ontario-landlord-guide).

### 4. Move-In and Move-Out Procedures

Most condo corporations require landlords to book the elevator or freight entrance for move-ins and move-outs, often with a refundable deposit.

This is generally enforceable against you as the owner. The corporation can charge you administrative fees or damages if you don't follow their procedures.

However, the corporation cannot charge the **tenant** directly for these fees or add them to the rent. Any cost the condo imposes on you as a result of your tenant's move-in must be absorbed by you — not passed to the tenant as a rent addition or separate fee, because the RTA prohibits charging tenants anything beyond the rent and certain permitted charges.

### 5. Common Area Rules and Conduct

Noise, amenity use, smoking policies, parking, locker use — condo corporations regulate all of this through their rules.

These rules bind your tenant because you gave them a copy before or at the start of the lease. If your tenant violates the condo rules — persistent noise complaints, unapproved use of amenities, smoking in prohibited areas — the condo corporation's enforcement mechanisms run against **you**, the owner.

You are responsible for your tenant's compliance with condo rules. If the condo fines you, you cannot simply pass that fine to the tenant as added rent. But you may have grounds to issue an N5 Notice to your tenant for substantially interfering with others' reasonable enjoyment — the basis for many noise and conduct complaints.

### 6. Short-Term Rentals

Many condo corporations have added explicit bans on short-term rentals — Airbnb-style rentals of less than 30 days.

The RTA only applies to tenancies. A short-term rental (under 30 days) is generally outside the RTA's scope and is governed by the Condominium Act and the condo's own rules.

If the condo bans short-term rentals, that restriction likely holds — it's not in conflict with the RTA because the RTA doesn't cover it. The City of London also has short-term rental licensing rules you need to check separately.

## Utilities: Who Pays What in a Condo Rental

Utility arrangements in condo rentals deserve special attention.

**Common element costs** — things like gas for heating common corridors, hydro for the lobby, condo fees — are your costs as an owner. They cannot be charged to the tenant as separate fees. Only lawful rent and permitted charges (parking, lockers, care services) are allowed.

However, if the unit has its own separate hydro meter, you can structure the lease so the tenant pays their own hydro directly — this is a lawful utility arrangement under the RTA.

**Condo fees are never the tenant's responsibility.** If your condo fees increase, you cannot pass that increase to the tenant mid-tenancy. Any increase in your carrying costs is absorbed until your next lawful rent increase.

## Rent Increases in a Condo Rental

The same rent increase rules apply to condo rentals as any other residential tenancy in Ontario.

For units **first occupied before November 15, 2018**: rent increases are subject to the annual rent increase guideline. For 2026, that guideline is 2.5%.

For units **first occupied after November 15, 2018**: the unit is exempt from rent control. You can raise the rent to any amount on a lease renewal, as long as you give proper 90-day written notice.

Most condos in Ontario's newer buildings — including many London developments built from 2018 onward — are rent-control exempt. This is a significant advantage for condo landlords building a rental portfolio.

The condo corporation's rules have no say in your rent increase. This is entirely governed by the RTA and [Ontario's rent increase guidelines](/blog/rent-increase-ontario-guidelines).

## Your Disclosure Obligations as a Condo Landlord

Under section 83 of the Condominium Act, you must give your tenant:

- A copy of the **declaration**
- A copy of the **bylaws**
- A copy of the **rules**

You must provide these **before or on the day the tenancy starts**. Keep a written record — have your tenant sign an acknowledgement that they received these documents.

If new rules are passed during the tenancy, you must give the tenant a copy **within 10 days** of receiving them yourself.

If you fail to provide these documents, the condo corporation's rules are still enforceable against you as the owner — but you have less ability to enforce them against your tenant.

## Common Mistakes Condo Landlords Make

**1. Assuming condo rules override the RTA**
They don't. Provincial law governs the tenancy. Condo rules bind you as an owner, not your tenant's RTA rights.

**2. Failing to provide condo documents before the lease starts**
This is a legal obligation under the Condominium Act. Keep signed acknowledgements from every tenant.

**3. Trying to pass condo fee increases to the tenant**
Condo fees are your cost of ownership. They cannot be added to rent outside of a lawful rent increase.

**4. Assuming you can evict for a condo rule violation alone**
Condo rule violations are not standalone grounds for eviction under the RTA. The violation must also constitute one of the specific grounds under the RTA — substantial interference, damage, illegal act.

**5. Letting the condo board deal directly with your tenant**
The condo corporation's legal relationship is with you, not your tenant. If the board has a complaint, it flows to you. You then address it with your tenant within the bounds of the RTA.

**6. Not checking the condo's documents before you buy**
If you're buying a unit to rent out, review the declaration and rules before closing. Some condo declarations restrict rentals entirely, or require board approval for leasing. Find out before you own it.

**7. Missing the condo's short-term rental prohibition**
If the declaration bans short-term rentals, Airbnb is not an option — regardless of what your lease says.

## A Practical Checklist for Condo Landlords

Before the tenancy starts:

- [ ] Read the full declaration, bylaws, and rules
- [ ] Give your tenant a copy of all three documents
- [ ] Have the tenant sign an acknowledgement of receipt
- [ ] Confirm whether the unit is rent-control exempt (first occupied after Nov 15, 2018)
- [ ] Check whether the condo's pet policy creates any leasing restrictions for you
- [ ] Confirm how utilities are metered — separate meter or shared?
- [ ] Check whether the condo requires notice for lease-signing or board approval

During the tenancy:

- [ ] Give your tenant copies of any new rules within 10 days of receiving them
- [ ] Handle board complaints — don't let the board contact your tenant directly
- [ ] Follow the RTA's entry notice requirements before any unit access, even for condo-required inspections
- [ ] Serve valid written notice for any rent increases

## FAQ

**Can a condo corporation refuse to allow my tenant to move in?**
No. Unless the condo's declaration requires board approval for leases (some do), the corporation cannot veto your choice of tenant. If approval is required, it must be given within a reasonable time and cannot be used to discriminate.

**Can the condo board evict my tenant?**
No. Only a landlord can apply to the Landlord and Tenant Board to evict a tenant. The condo corporation can pressure you to act, but cannot take action against your tenant directly.

**What if my tenant violates the condo's noise rules?**
The condo corporation can pursue you as the owner. You can then address the issue with your tenant. If the noise substantially interferes with other residents' reasonable enjoyment, you may be able to serve an N5 Notice under the RTA. See our post on [the N5 Notice](/blog/n5-notice-ontario) for the process.

**Can I add a lease clause saying the tenant must follow all condo rules?**
Yes — and you should. This is standard practice. Including a clause that requires the tenant to comply with the condo's declaration, bylaws, and rules is enforceable. It doesn't strip the tenant of their RTA rights, but it documents their obligation to follow the rules you gave them.

**What if the condo rules change during the tenancy?**
You must give your tenant a copy within 10 days of receiving them yourself. The tenant becomes bound by the new rules, subject always to their RTA rights.

**Are short-term rentals covered by the RTA?**
Generally no. Rentals under 30 days are outside the RTA's scope. If your condo bans short-term rentals, that ban is likely enforceable even if the RTA doesn't address it.

**What if the condo corporation raises special assessment fees — can I pass them to my tenant?**
No. Special assessments are costs you bear as an owner. They are not permitted to be charged to tenants beyond a lawful rent increase.

**My condo's rules ban smoking anywhere in the building. Does that override the RTA?**
Smoke-free policies are generally enforceable. Ontario law permits condominium corporations to establish smoke-free buildings. This is an area where condo rules and the RTA tend to align — persistent smoking that violates a declared smoke-free policy can support an N5 Notice on the basis of substantial interference.

## The Bottom Line

Renting out a condo in Ontario is not complicated — but it requires you to understand both legal frameworks and know which one takes priority when they conflict.

The short version:

- The RTA governs the tenancy. It wins when it conflicts with condo rules.
- Condo rules bind you as the owner. They can be passed on to your tenant through the lease.
- You are the bridge between the condo corporation and your tenant. Neither can bypass you.

The practical risk for condo landlords is being caught between two systems — a condo board demanding you enforce a rule the RTA limits your ability to enforce. Getting ahead of this requires knowing the rules before the tenancy starts, not after a conflict has already emerged.
