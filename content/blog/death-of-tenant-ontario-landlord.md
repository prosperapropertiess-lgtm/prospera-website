Most landlords never think about what happens when a tenant dies mid-tenancy — until it happens. When it does, almost every instinct you have will be wrong.

You cannot re-enter the unit immediately. You cannot keep the last month's rent deposit as compensation for lost rent. You cannot list the unit the next day. The tenancy does not automatically end when the tenant dies.

Ontario's *Residential Tenancies Act* treats the death of a sole tenant very differently from what most landlords expect. Getting this wrong exposes you to legal liability, LTB proceedings from the estate, and delays that drag on for months.

This guide explains what the law actually requires and what to do at each stage.

---

## The Core Rule: RTA Section 91

**Section 91 of the Residential Tenancies Act** governs what happens when a sole tenant dies. The key provisions:

1. The tenancy is **not terminated** by the tenant's death.
2. The tenancy continues until one of the following happens:
   - The estate gives the landlord 30 days' written notice to terminate, or
   - The landlord and the estate agree on a termination date.
3. The estate is responsible for paying rent until the tenancy ends.
4. The estate has the right to access the unit to remove the deceased tenant's belongings.

This surprises most landlords. The instinct is to treat death as the end of the tenancy. Under Ontario law, it is not.

The tenancy becomes the obligation of the estate — not of any individual heir or family member, unless they were already named on the lease.

---

## Sole Tenant vs. Multiple Tenants

Before any other step, determine how many people were on the lease.

**If the deceased was the only tenant on the lease:**
The rules in RTA s.91 apply. The tenancy does not automatically end, and the estate steps into the tenant's place. Everything in this guide applies.

**If other tenants were also named on the lease:**
The surviving co-tenants remain in the unit. Their tenancy continues exactly as before. RTA s.91 does not apply in this case. The surviving tenants owe rent in full, and the lease continues under its existing terms. The deceased tenant's share does not create a vacancy.

**If a family member or partner lived there but was not on the lease:**
This is legally complex. An unnamed occupant has no independent right to remain under the RTA. However, removing them is not as simple as changing the locks. If they dispute their status, the matter goes to the LTB. Do not act unilaterally. Get legal advice before serving any notice.

## What Happens Immediately After Death

When you learn a sole tenant has died, your first obligation is to **do nothing premature**. Specifically:

- Do not enter the unit to inspect, clean, or pack belongings.
- Do not accept rent from family members unless they were on the lease.
- Do not list the unit for rent.
- Do not change the locks — the estate still has the right to enter.

Your immediate steps:

### 1. Confirm the situation in writing

Send a brief written letter or email to the address of record acknowledging that you have been informed of the death and asking for contact information for the estate trustee (executor). Keep a copy. This starts your paper trail and signals that you are following a proper process.

### 2. Identify who is managing the estate

The estate trustee (executor) named in the will has authority over the tenancy. If there is no will, the matter goes to probate and an administrator is appointed by the court. Until an executor or administrator is legally confirmed, no one has clear authority to give the 30-day termination notice under RTA s.91.

In practice, a close family member often acts informally. The LTB expects reasonable cooperation between landlords and families in this situation, but formal termination notice must come from someone with actual authority.

### 3. Document the date you were notified

Write it down. The 30-day notice period and your rent entitlement calculations both run from specific dates. Your records matter here.

## The Estate's Right to Terminate

Under RTA s.91(1), the estate can end the tenancy by giving **written notice of at least 30 days** to the landlord. The 30-day period runs from the first day of the next rental period after the notice is given.

Example: The tenant dies on October 3. The estate gives written notice on October 15. The rent period starts the first of each month. The 30-day window runs from November 1, so the tenancy ends November 30.

The estate owes rent through the full 30-day period even if the unit is empty. This is the estate's obligation, not yours to absorb.

If the estate does not give notice, the tenancy technically continues month to month. Rent continues to accrue. In practice, most estates want to wrap things up quickly and will give notice as soon as they can.

### What if no one from the estate contacts you?

This happens. The tenant had no family locally, or the estate is slow to organize.

You cannot simply re-enter the unit after a few weeks. You will need to apply to the LTB — likely an L2 application (or in some cases an abandoned tenancy route under RTA s.2(1)) to get a proper order to terminate the tenancy. The LTB handles these situations.

If you believe the unit has been abandoned AND the tenant has died, document everything carefully before taking any steps. Read our guide on [abandoned tenancy rules in Ontario](/blog/abandoned-tenancy-ontario) for context, but be aware that a confirmed death with a known estate is not the same situation as an abandonment — get legal advice first.

## Rent and the Last Month's Rent Deposit

Rent continues to accrue until the tenancy legally ends. The estate owes it.

**The last month's rent deposit** was collected to cover the final month of tenancy. It is applied to the last month's rent when the tenancy ends — regardless of how the tenancy ends. You do not keep it as a penalty or to cover the cost of finding a new tenant.

The deposit must be returned to the estate minus the last month's rent it was applied against, along with any accrued deposit interest under RTA s.106(6). For more on how deposit interest is calculated, see our guide on [last month's rent deposit interest in Ontario](/blog/last-month-rent-interest-ontario).

If rent is owed beyond what the deposit covers, the estate owes the balance. Recovering it is a matter for Small Claims Court if the estate disputes it — the LTB does not collect from estates.

### What can you deduct from the deposit?

Only the last month's rent. Any unpaid rent beyond that, or damage to the unit beyond normal wear and tear, is a claim against the estate. The rules around what landlords can recover from deposits are covered in our guide on [what landlords can deduct from the last month's rent deposit](/blog/what-can-landlord-deduct-from-last-month-rent-ontario).

## Access to the Unit: The Estate's Right to Remove Belongings

The estate has the right to enter the unit to remove the deceased tenant's personal property. You must allow this access.

Practical steps:
- Agree on a specific date and time with the executor or family contact.
- Be present if possible, or have a property contact available.
- Do not remove or discard any belongings before the estate has had a chance to clear the unit.
- Document the condition of the unit before and after with photos and a written inspection report.

If the estate clears the unit but leaves belongings behind, you face a different set of rules about abandoned property. Do not throw things out without proper notice and documentation.

**Do not allow non-estate parties** — friends of the deceased, people who "knew" the tenant but have no legal authority — to remove property from the unit. The estate trustee controls what leaves the unit.

## The Move-Out Inspection

When the tenancy is ending, conduct a formal move-out inspection with the estate representative if possible. Use a written checklist. Photograph every room.

If the estate representative cannot attend, conduct the inspection in their absence and document it thoroughly. Send them a copy of your inspection report and photographs by email. Keep your records.

A move-out inspection done properly is how you support any damage claim against the estate. Without documentation, you have no legal standing for deductions beyond the last month's rent. Our guide on [move-in and move-out inspections in Ontario](/blog/move-in-move-out-inspection-ontario) covers the documentation standard in full.

## London, St. Thomas, and Strathroy: Local Considerations

If your rental unit is in London, it is registered under the City of London's Rental Housing Licensing program. The licence stays active regardless of a tenant death — the unit does not lose its licence, and there is no obligation to notify the city about the change in occupancy until you have a new tenant.

Once the tenancy formally ends and you are preparing to re-list:
- Update your records for the new tenancy before the new tenant moves in.
- Ensure your rental licence is current before re-advertising.
- Document the move-out inspection for your records and any future LTB proceedings.

In London, the London Regional LTB office handles any applications related to tenancy termination or estate disputes. Standard application timelines apply.

## 7 Common Mistakes Landlords Make

**1. Treating the death as immediate vacancy.**
The tenancy does not end on the date of death. Re-entering or relisting the unit before the tenancy is legally terminated exposes you to an LTB application from the estate.

**2. Accepting rent from unauthorized people.**
A family member who was not on the lease has no tenancy rights. Accepting their rent might create an implied tenancy with them. Do not accept rent from anyone who was not a named tenant without legal advice.

**3. Removing belongings without estate authorization.**
Even items that appear abandoned must be handled through the proper process. Removing the deceased tenant's property without estate consent is illegal.

**4. Keeping the last month's rent deposit as "compensation."**
The deposit belongs to the estate. It must be applied to the last month's rent and any overage returned, with interest. Keeping it creates a T1 application risk from the estate.

**5. Ignoring the estate because no one contacted you.**
Silence from the estate does not mean the tenancy ended. Rent continues to accrue. You need to proactively identify who is managing the estate and follow up in writing.

**6. Changing the locks before the tenancy ends.**
The estate has lawful access to the unit until the tenancy is terminated. Changing the locks constitutes an illegal lockout under RTA s.37(3) and can result in serious penalties.

**7. Not documenting everything.**
A tenant death creates unusual circumstances that can lead to disputes months later. Write down dates, keep correspondence, and photograph the unit thoroughly at every stage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does the tenancy automatically end when a tenant dies in Ontario?**
No. Under RTA s.91, the tenancy of a sole tenant does not end at death. It continues until the estate gives 30 days' written notice, or the landlord and estate agree on a termination date.

**Who pays rent after the tenant dies?**
The estate is responsible for rent until the tenancy legally ends. Rent accrues through the notice period even if the unit is empty.

**Can I re-enter the unit immediately after the tenant dies?**
No. The estate has the right to access the unit to remove belongings. You need the estate's cooperation before re-entering, and you cannot remove or discard property.

**What happens to the last month's rent deposit?**
It is applied against the last month of the tenancy. Any remaining balance is returned to the estate, with accrued deposit interest. You cannot keep it as compensation for the inconvenience.

**What if the tenant had no family or no will?**
The estate still exists — it just takes longer to organize. If no one from the estate contacts you after a reasonable period, you may need to apply to the LTB for a termination order. Get legal advice on the appropriate application.

**Can a family member take over the tenancy?**
Not automatically. A family member who was not on the lease has no RTA rights to the unit. If the estate and the family member both want the person to take over as a new tenant, you would need to sign a new lease with them — that is your choice as the landlord, not their right.

**How long does the process typically take in London?**
If the estate is organized and gives prompt 30-day notice, the tenancy can end in 4–6 weeks from the date of death. If the estate is slow to organize or there are disputes, 2–4 months is realistic. Factor this into your vacancy assumptions.

**Can the estate sublet or assign the tenancy while it continues?**
In theory, the estate has the rights of a tenant, which include assignment applications. In practice, estates rarely want to continue the tenancy and focus on terminating it quickly. If you receive a subletting or assignment request from an estate, get legal advice.

## A Situation You Cannot Prepare for — But Can Handle Correctly

No landlord plans for this. When it happens, the instinct is to move quickly and treat the unit as available. That instinct will cost you.

The correct approach is slower and more procedural: confirm the tenancy continues, contact the estate, allow access, collect rent through the notice period, apply the deposit correctly, and document everything.
