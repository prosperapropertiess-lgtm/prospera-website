London, Ontario is not a flashy market. It doesn't get the press coverage that Toronto or Vancouver do. But for landlords focused on fundamentals — steady tenant demand, positive cash flow, and manageable entry prices — it has quietly become one of the more attractive mid-size cities in the province.

This guide covers what drives rental demand in London, how to analyze a property before you buy, which neighbourhoods produce the best returns, and what changes once you own a rental here. It's written for investors at any stage: people buying their first property, and people already owning one or two who want to understand the market better.

---

## Why London Works as a Rental Market

Three things drive consistent tenant demand in London, and none of them are going away.

**Western University and Fanshawe College.** Western enrolls roughly 35,000 students. Fanshawe has another 43,000. Many of these students rent off-campus — in houses near Western's main campus in the north end, in apartments near Fanshawe's east-end campus, or anywhere with reasonable transit access. Student demand peaks each spring and fills vacancies quickly if a unit is priced and listed correctly.

**Healthcare and hospital employment.** London Health Sciences Centre is one of the largest employers in the city. St. Joseph's Health Care adds another major anchor. Hospital workers — nurses, technicians, administrative staff — are stable long-term tenants. They're not seasonal. They renew leases. They have consistent income. Properties near University Hospital and Victoria Hospital tend to attract this tenant profile.

**Population growth.** London's population crossed 500,000 in the early 2020s and has continued to grow. Much of that growth has come from interprovincial migration and newcomers to Canada — people who often rent before buying. That underlying demand keeps vacancy rates low.

**Vacancy rates.** London's rental vacancy rate has stayed below 2% in recent years. In practical terms: a well-priced unit in good condition rents quickly. Prospera's leasing data shows average fill times of 2–4 weeks for properties that are priced correctly and listed with proper photos. Compare that to a city with a 5–6% vacancy rate, where a unit might sit for two or three months.

## What the Cash Flow Actually Looks Like

Cash flow is what remains after all expenses are paid. A property can look attractive on paper — good location, decent condition, rising rents — but still lose money each month if the expenses aren't accounted for honestly.

Here are realistic numbers for a London 2-bedroom unit purchased in 2026.

**Income:**

| Unit type | Typical monthly rent (2026) |
|---|---|
| Bachelor / studio | $1,200–$1,450 |
| 1 bedroom | $1,550–$1,850 |
| 2 bedroom | $1,900–$2,200 |
| 3 bedroom | $2,200–$2,700 |
| 4 bedroom (student house) | $2,800–$3,400 |

A 2-bedroom unit at $2,100/month generates $25,200/year in gross rent.

**Expenses (typical for a 2BR owned property):**

| Expense | Estimated annual amount |
|---|---|
| Property tax | $3,800–$5,200 |
| Insurance | $1,400–$2,000 |
| Maintenance and repairs | $1,500–$2,500 (1–1.5% of purchase price) |
| Utilities (if included) | $0–$2,400 |
| Vacancy allowance (5%) | $1,260 |
| Property management (if applicable) | $2,016–$2,520 (8–10%) |
| Mortgage interest (varies by down payment and rate) | Variable |

Total non-mortgage expenses typically run $8,000–$12,000/year for a standard rental unit with no utilities included. At a $25,200 gross income, that leaves $13,000–$17,000 before mortgage costs.

Whether you're cash-flow positive depends almost entirely on your financing. A property purchased with 20% down at current rates will have a different break-even point than one refinanced from equity in another property. Run your own numbers using your actual rate, your down payment, and the specific unit's tax and insurance costs — not averages from another investor's spreadsheet.

**The right way to evaluate a property:** calculate your gross yield first, then stress-test it.

Gross yield = (annual rent / purchase price) × 100

A $450,000 property renting for $2,100/month:
- Annual rent: $25,200
- Gross yield: ($25,200 / $450,000) × 100 = **5.6%**

London properties in well-located neighbourhoods typically yield 4.5–6.5% gross. Below 4%, cash flow is difficult without significant appreciation expectations. Above 6.5%, the property either has above-market rents, deferred maintenance, or both — look carefully at why.

## London Neighbourhoods: What Each Offers Investors

The right neighbourhood depends on your goals: higher yield with more tenant turnover, or lower yield with longer, more stable tenancies. Neither is wrong — they just require different management approaches.

### Old North and Wortley Village

These are London's most desirable older neighbourhoods — walkable, close to downtown, established. Properties here hold value well and attract professional tenants who renew leases. Yields tend to be lower (4–5%) because purchase prices are higher relative to rents. Vacancies are rare.

**Good for:** landlords who want long, stable tenancies with minimal management. Not ideal if you're chasing yield.

### University District (near Western — north end)

Streets like Richmond, Huron, and Cheapside attract student renters. A house near Western can generate $2,800–$3,400/month by renting to three or four students on individual room agreements. Yields can run 6–8%. The trade-off: student tenancies turn over annually, summer vacancy risk is higher if rooms aren't pre-leased, and wear on units runs higher.

**Good for:** experienced landlords comfortable with annual turnover and tighter tenant management. Higher upside, higher involvement.

### East London (near Fanshawe College)

Properties east of Adelaide — near the Fanshawe campus and along Clarke Road, Hamilton Road, and Trafalgar Street — offer solid yields at lower purchase prices than the north end. Rents for a 3-bedroom in this area run $2,000–$2,500. Mixed demand: students, working families, newcomers.

**Good for:** investors buying at lower price points who want reasonable yields with a mixed tenant base. Less turnover than the Western area.

### South London (along Wellington, White Oaks, and Pond Mills)

Suburban, family-oriented, newer housing stock in some areas. Tenants tend to be families with children who stay 3–5 years. Lower yields (4–5%) but also lower maintenance needs and rare vacancy. Less management intensity.

**Good for:** investors who want set-it-and-forget-it stability. Good for out-of-town landlords who want stable cash flow without active involvement.

### Byron and Lambeth

Southwest London, often overlooked by investors. Quieter, suburban, slightly further from downtown. Properties in these neighbourhoods attract long-term tenants who value the neighbourhood over proximity to campus or downtown. Yield is modest but turnover is low. Move-out inspections are often uneventful.

**Good for:** landlords who prioritize low turnover above all else.

### Old East Village and Old South

Old East has gentrified significantly in the last decade. Proximity to downtown makes it increasingly attractive to younger professionals. Old South (between downtown and White Oaks) offers solid yields and a mix of tenants. Both areas have benefited from London's population growth.

## Single Unit vs. Multi-Family: Which Makes More Sense

Single-family rentals and multi-unit buildings (duplexes, triplexes, fourplexes) are fundamentally different investments. Neither is universally better.

**Single unit (renting a house or a condo):**
- Simpler to manage
- One lease, one tenant relationship, one set of maintenance calls
- Easier financing (conventional mortgage)
- Lower yield potential
- Total vacancy risk if the tenant leaves: 100% of income stops

**Multi-family (duplex, triplex, fourplex):**
- More complex: multiple tenants, multiple leases, more maintenance
- Higher gross income from a single property
- Vacancy diversification: if one unit goes vacant, others keep paying
- Higher purchase price but often better cap rate
- Financing is more complex above 4 units

A London duplex that rents for $2,100 upstairs and $1,750 downstairs generates $45,000/year. A single-family home in the same neighbourhood might rent for $2,400 and generate $28,800/year — and the purchase prices might be comparable.

If you're buying a multi-unit property that already has tenants, read the [buying property with existing tenants Ontario guide](/blog/buying-property-with-existing-tenants-ontario) before closing. The existing tenancy transfers to you automatically — lease terms, rent amount, and the previous landlord's obligations all come with it.

## The Regulatory Environment in London

London adds compliance layers that don't exist in smaller Ontario cities.

**Rental Housing Licensing.** The City of London requires a Rental Housing Licence for almost every rental property. This is not optional and is not automatically included with your purchase. The licence requires an initial inspection, an annual renewal, and ongoing compliance with property standards. If a property you buy already has a licence, confirm the address of record matches your mailing address — renewal notices go to the address on file, and out-of-town owners miss them.

Full details are in the [City of London rental unit licence guide](/blog/city-of-london-rental-unit-licence).

**Second units.** If a property has a basement apartment, it likely needs Second Unit Registration in addition to the Rental Housing Licence. These are two separate processes with different inspections. A basement apartment with no registration is non-compliant regardless of whether it's been occupied for years. See [City of London second unit registration](/blog/city-of-london-second-unit-registration).

**Property standards.** London's Property Standards By-law runs parallel to the RTA. Property Standards Officers enforce it on a complaint-driven basis. An outstanding Order to Comply blocks your Rental Housing Licence renewal. See [London Ontario property standards bylaw](/blog/london-ontario-property-standards-bylaw).

**Rent control.** Units first occupied on or after November 15, 2018 are exempt from the rent increase guideline — you can raise rent to any amount with proper notice. Units first occupied before that date are subject to the annual guideline (2.5% in 2026). If a property you're buying has older units and current rents significantly below market, confirm whether the units are rent-controlled before projecting future income. Details: [Ontario rent control exempt properties](/blog/ontario-rent-control-exempt-properties).

## Common Mistakes London Rental Investors Make

**1. Buying on gross rent, not net.** The $2,100/month tenant sounds great. Then you discover the unit has $2,200/year in property tax, $1,900/year in insurance, $800/year in maintenance history, and a soft water heater that's nine years old. Run every expense before you commit.

**2. Ignoring the Rental Housing Licence requirement.** Out-of-province buyers and investors who've only owned in smaller Ontario cities often don't know about it. Operating without a licence triggers fines up to $25,000 per day. London's enforcement has increased.

**3. Assuming a student house is easy income.** High yield is real. But student houses require a management system: strict screening, a clear move-out process each August, fast turnaround for September, and tolerance for above-average wear. Some landlords run student houses profitably for years. Others burn out after one cycle.

**4. Miscounting the compliance obligations.** For a London property, you may need to track: annual rent increase N1 form, last-month-rent deposit interest (owed annually under RTA s.106(6)), Rental Housing Licence renewal, move-in and move-out inspection documentation, and maintenance records. Missing any of these creates LTB exposure. See [landlord record keeping Ontario](/blog/landlord-record-keeping-ontario) for a system.

**5. Waiting too long to set up systems.** The landlord who buys their third property before building a record-keeping system eventually faces a situation — an LTB filing, a disputed move-out, a missed renewal — that reveals the gap. The cost of that gap is always higher than the cost of systems.

**6. Underestimating vacancy risk.** A 5% vacancy allowance is standard in property analysis. In London's tight market, many landlords never experience that. But one difficult tenant — one who goes to the LTB, one who damages a unit — can create 2–3 months of vacancy or worse. Budget for it.

**7. Not factoring management costs.** If you're self-managing, your time has value. If you're ever going to hire management, the fee is 8–10% of gross rent. Run your numbers both ways: can the investment still work with a management fee? If not, the margin is thin. See [property management fees Ontario](/blog/property-management-fees-ontario) for fee structures.

## When to Bring in a Property Manager

Many investors self-manage their first property. It's a reasonable choice — you learn the market, the tenant relationship, and the compliance obligations. But there are situations where professional management adds more value than it costs.

**Distance.** London's entry notice rules (24 hours written notice before non-emergency entry) are unchanged regardless of where you live. A landlord in another city cannot respond to maintenance calls the way a local owner can. Out-of-province and out-of-region investors almost always benefit from professional management. The Rental Housing Licence renewal notice alone — which goes to your address of record — has created real problems for absent owners.

**Scale.** Managing a second property often feels manageable. A third and fourth change the math. The compliance obligations multiply: four annual N1 notices, four deposit interest calculations, four licence renewals. The time required begins to exceed what most people with full-time employment can absorb. At four properties, the management fee typically costs less than the landlord's time.

**After an LTB experience.** One difficult tenancy — especially one that reaches the LTB — changes many landlords' calculus. The filings, the documentation requirements, the hearing preparation, the Sheriff enforcement timeline — all of it takes significant time and carries real error risk. A property manager handles this as a routine process. For a landlord, it's often a first encounter with a system they didn't fully understand.

**The freedom calculation.** The real question isn't whether you can manage the property yourself. It's whether you want to. The management fee on a $2,100/month London property is $168–$210/month. If that buys your evenings and weekends back, the math isn't purely financial.

If you're unsure whether management makes sense for your situation, the [property manager vs self-managing Ontario guide](/blog/property-manager-vs-self-managing-ontario) walks through the break-even analysis in detail.

## A Practical Due Diligence Checklist

Before buying a London rental property, confirm:

- [ ] Current rent vs. market rate — is there upside, or is it already above market?
- [ ] Rent-controlled or exempt? (first occupancy date vs. November 15, 2018)
- [ ] Rental Housing Licence status — does one exist? What's the address of record?
- [ ] Any outstanding Orders to Comply from Property Standards?
- [ ] Second unit registration (if basement apartment) — completed or not?
- [ ] Last month's rent deposit on file — and is deposit interest current?
- [ ] Existing lease terms — fixed or month-to-month, when does it expire?
- [ ] Maintenance history — appliance ages, plumbing, heating system
- [ ] Insurance — will your insurer cover a tenanted property from day one?
- [ ] Zoning — is the existing use permitted? Are you in a zone that allows multi-unit?

For tenanted purchases, the existing lease transfers to you on closing. You cannot ask the tenant to re-sign a new lease, negotiate new rent terms, or require a new last month's deposit. What's in place is what you inherit.

## Common Questions from London Rental Investors

**How long does it take to fill a vacancy in London?**
Prospera's data shows 2–4 weeks for a correctly priced, properly listed unit. DIY landlords who price above market or delay listing typically wait 5–9 weeks. Late fall and winter are slower; spring through August are the fastest months. More detail: [how long does it take to find a tenant London Ontario](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-find-tenant-london-ontario).

**Can I raise rent on a new tenant after the previous one leaves?**
Yes. When a tenancy ends, you can set the rent at market rate for the next tenant regardless of what the previous tenant was paying. This is called vacancy decontrol. Rent guideline restrictions apply once a tenancy has started, not to the initial rent set with a new tenant.

**Do I need a property manager right away?**
Not necessarily. Many landlords successfully manage their first property without professional help, especially if they have local access and some familiarity with the RTA. Where most first-time investors struggle is in the compliance obligations — rent increase timing, deposit interest, proper N4 service — not the day-to-day calls. Starting with a clear record-keeping system goes a long way.

**What's the rent control situation for new properties?**
Units in buildings first occupied after November 15, 2018 are exempt from the annual guideline. If you're buying new construction or a post-2018 conversion, you have more flexibility on rent increases — but the N1 form, 90-day notice period, and once-per-12-month rule still apply.

**How do I know if a basement apartment is legal?**
In London, a legal basement apartment needs: a building permit and certificate (or a pre-existing unit that meets code), Second Unit Registration with the City, and a Rental Housing Licence. All three. A unit with any missing piece is non-compliant. See [basement apartment Ontario landlord guide](/blog/basement-apartment-ontario-landlord-guide) for the full checklist.

**Is London better than smaller markets like Strathroy or St. Thomas?**
It depends on your goals. London has deeper liquidity, stronger tenant demand, and faster fill times. Strathroy and St. Thomas have lower entry prices, lower competition from other investors, and simpler regulatory environments (no Rental Housing Licence requirement). Prospera operates in all three markets and the dynamics are meaningfully different. If you're choosing between markets, run the yield calculation for each specific property — generalizations only go so far.
