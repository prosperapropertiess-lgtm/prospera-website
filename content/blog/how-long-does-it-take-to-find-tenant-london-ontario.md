Every day a rental unit sits empty costs you money. The question every landlord asks when a tenant gives notice — or when they're listing for the first time — is: how long is this going to take?

The honest answer depends on what you're doing, when you're doing it, and how well you're doing it.

In London, Ontario, landlords who manage their own tenant search typically take **5–9 weeks** from vacancy to a signed lease. Landlords who use a professional tenant placement service typically take **2–4 weeks** for the same result.

That gap — three to five weeks — adds up to real money. On a typical London two-bedroom at $2,100/month, each extra week of vacancy costs you $525. A five-week difference is over $2,600 in lost rent.

This guide walks through every phase of the tenant placement process, what drives timelines, what slows them down, and when professional placement makes sense for your situation.

## The London Rental Market: What to Expect in 2026

London, Ontario has a tighter rental market than Toronto or the GTA, but it's not without competition. The city draws tenants from several different groups: Western University students, Fanshawe College students, young professionals working in healthcare and manufacturing, families relocating from the GTA for affordability, and retirees downsizing.

Each group has different timing. Students search in January–March for September move-ins. Young professionals and families move year-round, with peaks in May–June and August–September. Retirees tend to move slowly and prefer winter months.

**What this means for your timeline:** If your unit is vacant in July, you'll see strong demand. If it's vacant in November, you'll see less. Your timeline in the slow season — November through January — may be 30–50% longer than in peak months.

London's vacancy rate for purpose-built rentals has stayed in the 2–3% range in recent years. That's not a crisis, but it's not a landlord's market either. Good units get leased in weeks. Units with problems — poor photos, overpriced, maintenance issues, slow response to inquiries — sit.

## Phase-by-Phase Timeline: What Each Step Actually Takes

Whether you're managing the search yourself or using a property manager, tenant placement moves through the same phases. The time each phase takes is where the differences show up.

### Phase 1: Getting the Unit Ready (1–2 weeks)

Before any photos or listings, the unit needs to be clean, repaired, and presentable. This means:

- Final inspection after the outgoing tenant leaves
- Touch-up paint, patching holes, cleaning appliances
- Any deferred maintenance that would show up in a viewing
- Professional cleaning if needed

DIY landlords often take 10–14 days because they're coordinating contractors around their own work schedule. A property manager with a regular maintenance network can compress this to 3–5 days.

### Phase 2: Photography and Listing (2–5 days)

The listing is your first impression. Units with dark, blurry smartphone photos receive 40–60% fewer qualified inquiries than units with proper photography.

A professional property manager shoots the unit, writes compliant listing copy (no tenant descriptions — see the Ontario Human Rights Code), and distributes the listing to MLS, Rentals.ca, Kijiji, Facebook Marketplace, and secondary platforms in one day.

DIY landlords typically post to one or two platforms, often with their phone camera, and revisit and revise the listing several times over 3–5 days before it performs.

### Phase 3: Inquiry Management and Showings (1–3 weeks)

This is where DIY timelines stretch the most.

When inquiries come in, qualified applicants expect a response within a few hours. A landlord with a full-time job who checks their email at 7 p.m. loses leads to faster-responding properties. Professional management responds to inquiries the same day and books showings in concentrated blocks.

Showings for a well-priced London unit typically run for 1–2 weeks before you've seen enough applicants to choose. A poorly priced unit or one listed on a single platform can stay in showings for four or five weeks with nothing viable.

### Phase 4: Screening (3–7 days)

A proper tenant screening includes: credit check (Equifax or TransUnion), income and employment verification (recent pay stubs, employer letter), reference calls from previous landlords, and an interview.

Done carefully, this takes 3–7 days depending on how responsive applicants are. Many landlords shortcut this step — they do a quick credit check and take the first applicant who seems friendly — and pay for it later.

For more on what to look for (and what to avoid) in the screening process, see [tenant screening red flags](/blog/tenant-screening-red-flags).

### Phase 5: Lease Preparation and Signing (2–5 days)

Ontario requires a Standard Lease (Form 006) for all new residential tenancies. The lease must be prepared, reviewed, signed by both parties, and the last month's rent deposit collected before the tenancy begins.

A property manager prepares this document the same day the applicant is approved. DIY landlords often take several days to draft and exchange the lease, especially if they're using an online template they need to customize.

### Phase 6: Move-In and Inspection (half a day)

A thorough move-in inspection — with photographs of every room — protects both parties. This is a legal requirement in Ontario and forms the basis of any future claim for damage deductions. See [move-in and move-out inspection Ontario](/blog/move-in-move-out-inspection-ontario) for the full process.

## What Slows a Tenant Search Down

These are the most common reasons London landlords extend their vacancy period by two to four weeks beyond necessary.

### 1. Overpricing the unit

This is the single biggest driver of long vacancies. If your two-bedroom in Old North is listed at $2,400 when the neighbourhood range is $2,050–$2,200, you'll see inquiries from people who can't qualify and silence from people who can.

Price is not about what you need — it's about what similar units in your specific London neighbourhood are renting for right now. See [how much to charge for rent in London, Ontario](/blog/how-much-charge-rent-london-ontario) for current ranges by bedroom count and area.

### 2. Poor listing photos

Units with good photography fill faster — not because photos trick anyone, but because they communicate that the landlord takes care of their property. A dark, cluttered photo signals problems a tenant doesn't want to inherit.

### 3. Listing on one platform only

Kijiji alone is not sufficient in 2026. MLS via a real estate agent or property manager expands reach significantly. Rentals.ca and Facebook Marketplace add younger renter demographics. Posting to all four platforms is a one-hour task with substantial yield.

### 4. Slow response to inquiries

Qualified tenants are looking at multiple properties simultaneously. If you take 12–24 hours to respond to an inquiry, many of them will have already committed elsewhere. The expected response window is 2–4 hours.

### 5. No-shows and scheduling friction

Allowing applicants to book individual showings one at a time creates dead pockets and no-shows. Blocking two concentrated showing periods — for example, Tuesday evening and Saturday morning — generates better attendance and lets you compare applicants.

### 6. Incomplete screening

Accepting a tenant without completing income verification creates risk. Landlords who take the first pleasant-seeming applicant without running a credit check and calling references take on a higher proportion of non-paying or problem tenants. This isn't relevant to timeline — until it is, when you're six months into a problematic tenancy and starting an N4 notice process.

### 7. Lease delays

A lease that takes a week to prepare and exchange — because both parties keep emailing revisions — costs days. A prepared lease and a clear timeline (sign by X date or we move to the next applicant) keeps momentum.

### 8. Listing during the slow season without adjusting strategy

If you're listing in November, you need to either accept a slightly longer timeline, price slightly below the spring peak, or invest in more active promotion. The same approach that fills a unit in two weeks in June can take six weeks in December.

## What Speeds a Tenant Search Up

These factors consistently reduce time-to-fill for London rental properties.

**1. Professional-quality photography.** This alone reduces average inquiry time by one to two weeks by increasing qualified applicant volume.

**2. Competitive pricing from day one.** Price correctly at listing — not "let's see" and reduce later. Each price reduction wastes a week.

**3. Multi-platform distribution on day one.** MLS, Rentals.ca, Kijiji, and Facebook Marketplace posted simultaneously.

**4. Same-day inquiry response.** Even an auto-reply that says "we'll be in touch within 2 hours" keeps applicants from moving on.

**5. A completed application package.** Having a written application form, a list of what's required (pay stubs, government ID, reference contacts), and a clear timeline communicated upfront cuts screening time in half.

## Common Mistakes That Extend Your Vacancy

These are the patterns that add two to six weeks to a tenant search unnecessarily.

**Waiting to list until the unit is 100% ready.** You can list before the unit is available and screen tenants simultaneously with final repairs. A "move-in ready on [date]" listing performs well if the date is within three to four weeks.

**Showing to anyone who books.** Pre-qualifying applicants with two or three screening questions before booking a showing — income range, move-in date, number of occupants — filters unqualified viewers and saves everyone's time.

**Accepting partial information during screening.** If an applicant can't provide a pay stub, a reference from their last landlord, or a government-issued ID, they're not ready to be screened. Waiting for them to produce these documents days later kills momentum.

**Letting the showing period drag without a deadline.** Tell applicants that the unit is available and you're accepting applications until a specific date. This creates urgency and reduces the chance that a good applicant waits indefinitely.

**Not using a property manager's network.** Established property managers in London carry a roster of pre-screened applicants looking for units. A unit that might take four weeks on public platforms can be filled in one to two weeks from this internal list.

## FAQ

**How long does it typically take to find a tenant in London, Ontario?**

For DIY landlords, the typical range is 5–9 weeks from vacancy to a signed lease. Professional tenant placement services in London typically achieve 2–4 weeks for the same result, because they operate full-time, use professional photography, list on all major platforms simultaneously, and have an established applicant network.

**What time of year is the best to list a rental in London?**

May through August is peak demand in London, driven by students, families relocating before the school year, and the general spring real estate cycle. November through January is the slowest period. If you can avoid listing in late fall, your timeline will be shorter.

**Does professional tenant placement guarantee faster fill times?**

No guarantee is appropriate here. A difficult-to-fill unit (unusual layout, high price point, problematic location) will take longer regardless of who manages the placement. But a competent placement service eliminates the avoidable delays — slow inquiry response, weak photography, limited platform distribution — that extend DIY timelines by weeks.

**Is it faster to list through a real estate agent or a property management company?**

Both have access to MLS, which is the most valuable platform for London rentals. The difference is in ongoing process: a property management company is focused on tenant placement as a core service and handles screening, lease preparation, and move-in inspection as part of the process. A real estate agent may list on MLS but often hands off after an applicant is found.

**What's the vacancy rate in London, Ontario right now?**

London's purpose-built rental vacancy rate has been in the 2–3% range in recent years, which is moderate — not as tight as downtown Toronto but not a landlord's market where units fill in days. Good units at competitive prices fill quickly. Units with problems or at above-market prices sit for weeks.

**How much does vacancy really cost?**

At London's average two-bedroom rent of $2,100/month, each week of vacancy costs about $525 in lost rent — before carrying costs like mortgage interest, property tax, and utilities. A five-week vacancy is worth $2,625 in forgone rent. A professional placement service that cuts vacancy by four weeks typically covers its fee entirely from the saved vacancy time.

**What information should I collect from applicants to speed up screening?**

At minimum: full legal name, current address, move-in date needed, number of occupants (including children and pets), employment status and employer name, monthly income, last landlord contact information, and government-issued ID. Collecting this upfront — before booking a showing — filters unqualified applicants and reduces dead-end showings.

**Can I list a unit while the current tenant is still there?**

Yes, with conditions. Under the RTA, you must give the current tenant 24 hours' written notice before each showing, and showings must happen between 8 a.m. and 8 p.m. You cannot require the tenant to leave during showings. Most tenants cooperate, but the process is slower than showing a vacant unit. See your RTA obligations under [landlord entry rights in Ontario](/blog/landlord-entry-rights-ontario).
