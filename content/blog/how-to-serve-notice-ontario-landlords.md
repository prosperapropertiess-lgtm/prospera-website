You served your N4 notice. You waited. You filed your L1 application. Then, at the LTB hearing, the adjudicator dismisses your application.

Not because your rent arrears math was wrong. Not because the tenant had a defence. Because you handed the notice to the tenant's spouse instead of the tenant — and that method is not valid under the Residential Tenancies Act.

Service errors are one of the most common reasons eviction applications fail at the LTB. The RTA has specific rules about who can receive a notice, how it must be delivered, and when it is legally deemed to have been received. If you get any of those wrong, the notice is void. You have to start over.

This guide covers everything you need to know about serving notices correctly in Ontario.

---

## Why Service Rules Matter

Under the RTA, an eviction notice that was not properly served is not a valid notice. That means:

- The termination date on the notice has no legal effect
- Any LTB application based on that notice can be dismissed
- You lose the filing fee ($201 for online L1 applications)
- The timeline resets to zero — you serve again from day one
- If you are close to a lease renewal date or a seasonal vacancy, the delay can cost you months

The rules come from **RTA Section 191** and Ontario Regulation 516/06. They are not complicated, but they are exact. A minor deviation — serving at the wrong address, using the wrong method for the notice type, or miscounting the deemed-received window — can sink an otherwise solid application.

## Permitted Service Methods Under the RTA

The RTA allows six methods for serving documents on a tenant. Not every method is appropriate for every situation. Here is each method and when it applies.

### 1. Personal Service

You (or someone you authorize) hand the notice directly to the tenant — the named tenant, in person. This is the most reliable method and the one that eliminates any dispute about receipt.

**Deemed received:** Same day.

**Important:** Handing it to a roommate, a spouse, an adult child, or a neighbour is NOT personal service. The notice must go to the tenant specifically. If someone other than the tenant answers the door and the tenant is not present, personal service fails.

### 2. Mail

You send the notice by regular Canada Post mail to the rental unit address (or to another address the tenant has provided in writing as their mailing address).

**Deemed received:** 5 days after the postmarked date.

**Important:** This 5-day window is added to your termination date calculations. If an N4 requires 14 days' notice, and you mail it today, the tenant does not start counting their 14 days until 5 days from now. Plan accordingly — landlords who serve by mail and calculate the termination date from the mail date (rather than the deemed-received date) end up with a defective notice.

Registered or certified mail is not required — regular mail is sufficient. However, regular mail provides no proof of delivery. Keep the mailing receipt from Canada Post as evidence.

### 3. Courier

You send the notice by a licensed courier company to the rental unit address.

**Deemed received:** 1 day after the date of delivery to the courier.

**Important:** Same calculation warning as mail. If you deliver to the courier on September 1, the deemed received date is September 2.

### 4. Leaving in the Mailbox or Under the Door

You leave the notice in the tenant's mailbox, mail slot, or under the door of the rental unit.

**Deemed received:** The day it is left, if left before 5:00 p.m. If left after 5:00 p.m., deemed received is the following day.

**Important:** This method works for unit-specific delivery points. Leaving a notice at a general building mailbox that is not the tenant's designated box may not meet the standard. If you own a house or basement apartment, dropping it through the mail slot or under the front door is valid. Document the time — if you leave it at 4:45 p.m., it is received that day. At 5:15 p.m., it is received the next day. That one hour can affect whether your termination date is valid.

### 5. Fax

You fax the notice to a fax number the tenant has provided to you in writing.

**Deemed received:** The day the transmission is confirmed, if confirmed before 5:00 p.m. If confirmed after 5:00 p.m., deemed received is the following day.

In practice, very few tenants provide a fax number, and very few landlords have fax machines. This method is almost never used for residential tenancies.

### 6. Email

You send the notice to an email address the tenant has provided to you in writing as their address for service.

**Deemed received:** The day the email is sent, if sent before 5:00 p.m. If sent after 5:00 p.m., deemed received is the following day.

**Critical requirement:** The tenant must have provided the email address in writing specifically as their address for service. A tenant's email address in a screening application, on a text message chain, or on an old email thread does not automatically qualify. To use email reliably, have the tenant confirm their service email in writing — ideally in the lease itself. The Ontario Standard Lease has a field for this.

## Service Method Comparison Table

| Method | Deemed Received | Proof Available | Best Used When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal service | Same day | Yes — if witnessed | Tenant is present and accessible |
| Mail | 5 days after postmark | Canada Post receipt | Tenant access is difficult or relationship is tense |
| Courier | 1 day after courier pickup | Courier receipt | You need speed with documentation |
| Mailbox / Under door | Same day (before 5 p.m.) | Timestamp photo | Unit is accessible; quick delivery needed |
| Fax | Same day (before 5 p.m.) | Fax confirmation | Rarely applicable |
| Email | Same day (before 5 p.m.) | Email sent timestamp | Tenant confirmed email address in writing |

## Calculating the Termination Date Correctly

Every N-form notice has a required notice period. The termination date on the form must meet that minimum. The calculation starts from the **deemed received date**, not the date you served the notice.

Here are the minimum notice periods for the most common notices:

| Notice | Minimum Notice Period |
|---|---|
| N4 — Non-payment of rent | 14 days (monthly/yearly tenancy); 7 days (weekly/daily tenancy) |
| N5 — Interference with enjoyment / damage | 20 days (first notice); 14 days (second notice) |
| N6 — Illegal acts | 10 days |
| N7 — Serious damage | 10 days |
| N8 — Persistent late payment / no-cause end of fixed term | 60 days, end of period |
| N12 — Own use by landlord or purchaser | 60 days, end of period |
| N13 — Demolition or conversion | 120 days, end of period |

**Example:** You want to serve an N4 to a monthly tenant. You plan to leave the notice in the mailbox on October 1 at 4:00 p.m. The deemed received date is October 1. The 14-day period begins on October 2. The earliest valid termination date is October 15 — which must also fall on the last day of a rental period (or the last day of the fixed term). If the tenant's rent period runs the 1st to the last day of the month, October 31 is the valid termination date. Do not put October 15 on the form — that is not the last day of the period.

This is where most N4 termination date errors occur. The termination date must be both:
1. At least 14 days after the deemed received date
2. On or after the last day of a rent period

If the notice you have at hand is the [N4 notice](/blog/n4-notice-ontario), use that guide to confirm the exact date calculations for your tenancy type.

## Who Can Serve a Notice

You do not have to serve the notice yourself. Any of the following can serve on your behalf:

- You (the landlord)
- A property management company acting as your agent
- A licensed paralegal or lawyer
- Any authorized individual you designate

The person serving the notice does not need to be licensed. You can ask a trusted person to deliver it for you. What matters is that the method complies with RTA s.191 and that someone with knowledge of the delivery can attest to when and how it was served.

## Proving Service at the LTB

If the tenant disputes service at the LTB hearing, the adjudicator will ask how and when the notice was served. You need evidence.

The document you use to prove service is called a **Certificate of Service**. When you file an L1 or other LTB application, the application package includes a Certificate of Service form. You fill in the date, time, method, and address of service, and you sign it as a declaration.

Even before you file, keep your own record:

- **Personal service:** Note the date, time, and any witness present. A brief email to yourself confirming the delivery creates a timestamped record.
- **Mail:** Keep the Canada Post receipt. Photograph the envelope before sealing it.
- **Under door / mailbox:** Photograph the notice at the unit with a timestamp (your phone's camera has this). Send yourself a text noting the time.
- **Email:** Keep the sent email in your inbox. The email server timestamp is your evidence.

If the tenant claims they never received the notice and your Certificate of Service is incomplete or vague, the adjudicator may find service was not established.

## Serving Multiple Tenants on the Same Unit

If two or more tenants are on the lease, you must serve each one. One copy handed to one of two co-tenants is not valid service on the other tenant.

There are narrow exceptions — a tenant who has sublet the unit and is no longer in residence, for example — but as a rule, every named tenant on the lease gets their own served copy of the notice. Failing to serve all tenants means the untouched tenant can challenge the notice and may receive different LTB hearing rights.

---

## Addresses for Service

In most cases, you serve the notice at the rental unit address. However:

- If the tenant has provided a different address for service in writing, use that address
- If the tenant has abandoned the unit, you may need to serve the LTB application through the post at the rental unit even if no one is there; the LTB will accept a Certificate of Service showing mail to the last known address

Never serve a notice at a different address than the one on file without written confirmation from the tenant. If you know the tenant is temporarily staying elsewhere, personal service at their temporary address is valid — but service at an address they did not provide is not.

## 7 Common Mistakes That Void Service

1. **Serving the wrong person.** Handing an N4 to the tenant's spouse or roommate and counting it as served. Only personal service to the named tenant qualifies.

2. **Using email without written confirmation.** A tenant's email from Kijiji or a screening form does not make email a valid service address. Get a written confirmation — ideally in the lease.

3. **Miscounting mail days.** Calculating the notice period from the mail date instead of adding 5 deemed-delivery days first. Results in a termination date that is 5 days too early.

4. **Not accounting for the 5 p.m. rule.** Leaving a notice in the mailbox at 5:30 p.m. and counting that day as the received day. Service after 5 p.m. shifts the deemed received date to the following day.

5. **Skipping the Certificate of Service.** Filing an L1 application without completing the Certificate of Service, or completing it with vague information. If the tenant disputes service, a blank certificate loses.

6. **Serving only one of two co-tenants.** When two people are on the lease, both get served. No exceptions.

7. **Using personal service by proxy.** Asking a neighbour to hand the notice to the tenant and treating it as personal service. Only the person named as the tenant can accept personal service.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I serve an N4 by text message?**
No. Text message is not an approved service method under the RTA. Email is valid only if the tenant has provided an email address in writing as their service address. A text exchange is not the same thing.

**What if the tenant refuses to accept the notice?**
If the tenant refuses to take the document during a personal service attempt, the attempt does not count as service. Try again, or switch to a different method (mail, under the door). Leaving the notice on the ground in front of a tenant who refused it is not valid service.

**Can I email an N4 to a tenant who gave me their email for other purposes (maintenance requests, for example)?**
Using that email for service is legally risky. The RTA requires the tenant to provide their email address as their address for service. An email used for maintenance coordination may not meet that standard. To be safe, have the tenant confirm in writing that they accept that address for RTA notices.

**Does the Certificate of Service need to be notarized?**
No. It is a declaration, not an affidavit. You sign it under the affirmation that the information is true. No notary or commissioner of oaths is required.

**I served by mail but lost the receipt. Can I still prove service?**
You can testify to the service at the hearing, but without the receipt you are relying on your word against the tenant's. Courts and tribunals can accept oral testimony, but a receipt eliminates the dispute entirely. Going forward, always photograph the envelope and receipt before mailing.

**The tenant says they never got the notice. The adjudicator ruled against me. Can I serve again?**
Yes. A failed LTB application due to defective service does not prevent you from starting over. Serve the new notice correctly, file a fresh application, and pay the filing fee again. The original notice is void; you cannot amend it.

**My tenant has moved out but still owes rent. Where do I serve the L1?**
If the tenancy has ended but you are still filing for outstanding arrears, serve the application at the last known address. For an L1 where the tenancy is ongoing, serve at the rental unit.

**Can a licensed paralegal serve on my behalf?**
Yes. Many London-area paralegals who handle LTB matters will manage the service documentation as part of their representation. If you are paying $400–$1,200 for paralegal representation, confirm that service documentation is included in their scope.

## Serving Notices Is a Technical Step With Real Consequences

Most of the strategic work of a successful LTB application happens before you file — in the notice itself, the documentation you build, and the evidence you prepare. But service is the mechanical step that holds it all together. A well-documented N4 on the wrong date, sent by the wrong method, to the wrong person, is legally worthless.

If you have served a notice and are unsure whether the service was valid, compare what you did against the methods in this guide before you file. If there is any doubt, re-serve using personal service or the under-the-door method and document it clearly.
