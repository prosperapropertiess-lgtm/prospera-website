Capital cost allowance — CCA — is one of the most misunderstood deductions available to Ontario landlords. Used correctly, it defers tax on rental income. Used without understanding the full picture, it creates a large, unexpected tax bill when you sell.

This guide explains what CCA is, how it applies to rental properties, what you can and cannot depreciate, how to calculate it, and the recapture risk that stops many accountants from recommending it at all.

---

## What Is Capital Cost Allowance?

Capital cost allowance is the CRA's system for depreciating capital assets — things you buy that last more than one year and decline in value over time. For rental property owners, that mainly means the building itself, plus major appliances, fixtures, and furnishings.

You cannot deduct the full cost of a capital asset in the year you buy it. Instead, you deduct a percentage of its remaining value each year. That percentage, and the category of asset, is called a CCA class.

CCA is claimed on Schedule 8 of your T1 return and flows through your T776 — Statement of Real Estate Rentals — as a deduction against rental income. It reduces your net rental income for the year, which reduces the tax you owe.

What it does not do is make the asset cheaper. It defers tax. When you eventually sell the property, the CRA claws back every dollar of CCA you claimed through a mechanism called recapture.

---

## CCA Classes for Rental Property

Different assets are grouped into classes. Each class has its own depreciation rate. For rental property, the main ones are:

| CCA Class | Asset | Rate | Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Class 1 | Most residential rental buildings (built after 1987) | 4% | Declining balance |
| Class 3 | Older brick/concrete buildings (built before 1988) | 5% | Declining balance |
| Class 6 | Frame/wood construction (older structures) | 10% | Declining balance |
| Class 8 | Appliances, furniture, equipment | 20% | Declining balance |
| Class 10 | Vehicles used for rental management | 30% | Declining balance |
| Class 13 | Leasehold improvements | Straight-line | Term + 1 year |

**Most landlords in London, St. Thomas, and Strathroy with post-1987 construction will use Class 1 at 4%.**

The 4% rate is applied to the declining balance — meaning you take 4% of whatever the remaining cost is each year, not 4% of the original purchase price. The balance declines every year you claim, so your annual CCA deduction shrinks over time.

---

## Land Is Never Depreciable

Before calculating anything, subtract the land value from your property purchase price. You can only depreciate the building, not the land underneath it.

You need to allocate the purchase price between land and building. There is no universal formula. Common approaches:

- Use the property tax assessment: most Ontario municipalities separate land and building values on the MPAC assessment. The ratio is often used as a reasonable allocation.
- Use an appraisal that separates values.
- Apply a flat rule with professional advice (many accountants use 70-80% building / 20-30% land for urban Ontario, depending on property type and location).

**Example:** You buy a duplex in London for $550,000. The MPAC assessment shows 72% building value. Your depreciable cost is $396,000.

---

## The Half-Year Rule

In the year you acquire a property, you can only claim half your normal CCA. This is called the half-year rule (also called the 50% rule).

**Example using Class 1 at 4%:**

- Purchase price: $550,000
- Land allocation (28%): $154,000
- Building (depreciable): $396,000
- Year 1 CCA (half-year): $396,000 × 4% × 50% = **$7,920**
- Undepreciated Capital Cost (UCC) end of Year 1: $396,000 − $7,920 = $388,080
- Year 2 CCA (full year): $388,080 × 4% = **$15,523**
- UCC end of Year 2: $388,080 − $15,523 = $372,557

Your UCC is the remaining depreciable balance at any point in time. You track it on Schedule 8 every year.

---

## The CCA Restriction: You Cannot Create a Rental Loss

This is the rule that surprises most landlords who discover CCA.

You can only claim CCA up to the amount that reduces your net rental income to zero. You cannot use CCA to push your rental income into a loss.

**Example:**

- Gross rental income: $26,400
- Operating expenses (taxes, insurance, maintenance, management): $14,800
- Net income before CCA: **$11,600**
- Maximum CCA claim this year: $11,600 (you cannot go further)

Even if your calculated CCA entitlement is $15,000, the CRA will only let you claim $11,600 this year. The unclaimed balance is not lost — it stays on your Schedule 8 as unused UCC and is available in future years when you have more rental income.

This restriction applies per property (or per pooled class). It is different from other deductions like mortgage interest, which can create a rental loss.

---

## Recapture: The Deferred Tax Bill

Recapture is the reason many accountants advise against claiming CCA at all.

When you sell your rental property, the CRA compares what you receive for the building to the UCC at the time of sale:

- If your proceeds exceed the UCC → **recapture** — the difference is added to your income in the year of sale at your full marginal tax rate.
- If your proceeds are less than the UCC but above zero → **terminal loss** — you get a deduction (this is rare in appreciating Ontario markets).
- If your proceeds exceed the original capital cost → the excess is a capital gain, taxed separately at a 50% inclusion rate.

**Recapture example:**

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Original building cost | $396,000 |
| Total CCA claimed over 10 years | $62,400 |
| UCC at time of sale | $333,600 |
| Sale proceeds allocated to building | $560,000 |
| Recapture (proceeds − UCC) | $226,400 |
| Tax at 46% marginal rate | ~$104,000 |

That $104,000 tax bill is entirely the result of CCA deductions that temporarily saved you money on your T1 each year. The savings were real but temporary. The recapture collects them back — often in a year when you have already received sale proceeds and may not be expecting a large tax liability.

Capital gains are taxed differently and partially. Recapture is not a capital gain — it is ordinary income, taxed at your full marginal rate.

---

## Terminal Loss

A terminal loss occurs when you sell and your proceeds are less than your UCC (but more than zero). In that scenario, you can deduct the difference as a terminal loss against any source of income in the year of sale — not just rental income.

Terminal losses are uncommon in London and surrounding area given the appreciation trend over the past decade, but can apply in distress sales, rural properties, or cases where the building itself has significantly declined in value while land value held.

## When Claiming CCA Makes Sense

Whether to claim CCA is not a universal answer. It depends on your tax situation and when you plan to sell.

**CCA makes sense when:**
- You have significant rental income to shelter and expect to hold the property long-term (20+ years)
- You are in a high marginal tax bracket and the annual saving is material
- You are in a year with unusually high rental income (a vacancy gap closed, above-normal rent collected) and want to reduce the spike
- You are planning to hold through retirement and expect to sell in a lower income year, reducing the recapture tax rate
- You are a corporation with different tax treatment (consult an accountant)

**CCA should be approached carefully when:**
- You plan to sell within 5–10 years — the recapture will likely exceed the deductions
- You are already in a low marginal tax bracket — the annual saving is modest
- The property has appreciated significantly — the recapture exposure grows with every dollar claimed
- You have a capital gain already coming — combining capital gain + recapture can push you into a high bracket

The right decision depends on your exit timeline, expected sale price, and income in the year of sale. A licensed accountant who understands rental property is the right person to model this for your specific numbers.

## CCA on Appliances and Improvements (Class 8)

Appliances, furniture, and free-standing equipment in your rental unit are Class 8 assets — depreciated at 20% declining balance. This includes:

- Refrigerators, stoves, washers, dryers
- Portable air conditioners
- Furniture in a furnished unit
- Standalone storage units, sheds
- Lawn mowers, snowblowers owned for property maintenance

The same half-year rule applies in year of acquisition.

**Important:** Improvements that are attached to the building — a new furnace, windows, roof, finished basement — are generally added to Class 1 (the building), not Class 8. They increase your UCC for the building, not a separate appliance class.

Repairs that maintain the existing state of the property (painting, fixing a broken pipe, patching drywall) are current expenses — deductible in full in the year incurred on the T776. The line between a repair and an improvement can be grey. The general rule: if it restores something to its original condition, it is a repair. If it makes it better than it was or replaces a whole component, it is a capital improvement and belongs on Schedule 8.

## London and SW Ontario Context

For landlords in London, St. Thomas, and Strathroy, a few local factors interact with CCA:

**Rental Housing Licence fees (London):** The City of London Rental Housing Licence fee is a current operating expense — it goes on the T776 as an "other expense," not on Schedule 8. It is not a capital cost. See [City of London Rental Housing Licence](/blog/city-of-london-rental-unit-licence) for the current fee schedule.

**New construction (post-Nov 2018):** If you purchased a new-build unit first occupied after November 15, 2018, it is exempt from Ontario's rent increase guideline. This can mean higher rent income, which makes the CCA restriction less binding — you are more likely to have income to shelter. However, the recapture risk is the same.

**Cash flow vs. tax deferral:** London 2BR market rent is roughly $2,100–$2,300/month as of 2026. At that level, a fully loaded $500,000 property often runs near-zero or slightly negative cash flow after financing. CCA does not help cash flow — it only helps your T1 tax bill. Do not confuse the two.

**Multiple properties:** If you own more than two units, your Class 1 pool may include multiple buildings. CCA is calculated on the pool balance, not each building separately — but you should track each building's UCC for sale purposes. This is an area where professional bookkeeping pays for itself quickly.

## 7 Common Mistakes Ontario Landlords Make with CCA

**1. Claiming CCA on the land**
Land is not depreciable. If you include land in your Class 1 balance, every dollar of CCA is an error that the CRA can reassess.

**2. Forgetting the half-year rule in year of acquisition**
Claiming the full 4% in year one instead of 2% is a common T1 error. The CRA applies the half-year rule automatically and will adjust your Schedule 8.

**3. Not tracking UCC year by year**
If you do not track your Schedule 8 carefully, you will not know your recapture exposure when you sell. Many landlords are blindsided at closing.

**4. Treating CCA as free money**
CCA defers tax, it does not eliminate it. Every dollar you claim increases your recapture exposure by that dollar. The annual benefit is temporary.

**5. Claiming CCA in a year with little rental income**
If your net income before CCA is small, the annual benefit is small and the recapture risk grows. Run the numbers before claiming.

**6. Not separating repairs from capital improvements**
Calling a new furnace installation a "repair" is a CRA red flag. Capital improvements belong on Schedule 8, not as current expenses on the T776.

**7. Using CCA to cover a rental loss**
You cannot use CCA to push rental income negative. If you try, the CRA will simply disallow the excess. The deduction is only valid up to your net income.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the CCA rate for a residential rental building in Ontario?**
Most post-1987 residential rental buildings fall under Class 1 at 4% declining balance. Older buildings may qualify for Class 3 (5%) or Class 6 (10%). If you are unsure of your class, an accountant can confirm based on construction date and type.

**Can I claim CCA if my rental is operating at a loss?**
No. CCA can only reduce net rental income to zero, not create or deepen a loss. If your property is already losing money before CCA (after mortgage interest, taxes, insurance, and operating costs), CCA is not available that year.

**What happens to unclaimed CCA?**
It stays on your Schedule 8 as unused UCC. You can carry it forward and claim it in future years when you have rental income to offset.

**Is the whole purchase price depreciable?**
No. Only the building portion is depreciable. You must allocate the purchase price between land and building. Land is never depreciated.

**How is recapture taxed?**
Recapture is taxed as ordinary income — not at the capital gains rate. If your marginal rate is 46% in Ontario, recapture is taxed at 46%. This is significantly higher than the capital gains rate.

**Can I add the cost of a major renovation to my Class 1 balance?**
Yes. Capital improvements — a new roof, windows, HVAC system, finished basement — are added to your Class 1 UCC. They increase your depreciable balance and generate more CCA entitlement going forward.

**Do I have to claim CCA?**
No. CCA is optional each year. You choose how much to claim, up to the maximum. Many landlords choose to claim zero in years when they expect to sell soon or when the tax deferral benefit is not worth the recapture risk.

**What is a terminal loss?**
A terminal loss occurs when you sell a property for less than your remaining UCC. The shortfall is a deductible loss in the year of sale — claimable against any income. In appreciating Ontario markets this is uncommon, but it can arise in distress sales or on heavily depreciated properties.
